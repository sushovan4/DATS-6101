Lab 1

Getting messy data into shape, and asking who is missing from it.

How this session works

This lab is ungraded—the only one all semester. Nothing here counts. Its purpose is to show you what every other Monday looks like, while the stakes are zero.

Tonight is Part B only, at the keyboard, in fours, with a checkpoint in the middle. We take it slowly, because the point is the format rather than the finishing.

Part A is the spoken half—a pair mock interview—and it is how every Monday from Aug 31 opens. Tonight you see one question of it demonstrated from the front instead of running it yourselves. The other three are below, and they are worth working through with a partner before Aug 31, when this half starts carrying a point.

You may not use an AI assistant in any lab, including this one. The honest reason is in Part A, and it is worth reading even though we are not running it tonight: no interview lets you use one either.

Part A—Discussion

Not run tonight

A1 is demonstrated from the front; the rest are yours to practice. From Aug 31 this half runs in pairs at the start of every lab and is one of the three points for the session. Read the format below so that none of it is new then, and work A2 to A4 with someone before that.

Format: pair mock interview

Work in pairs. One plays the interviewer and holds the rubric card; the other answers out loud, without typing. Swap after each question, so that across the four you each ask twice and answer twice. Do not read the rubric card when you are the one answering.

Holding the rubric is not the easy seat. Knowing what a good answer contains is harder than producing one, and it is the skill that makes you useful in a code review.

Questions are labeled by role and stage—the kind of screen they belong to—never by company. Attributions to named firms are usually wrong, stale, or under NDA, and a wrong attribution misleads whoever is preparing.

The five-minute clock is part of the simulation. Real interview answers are time-boxed, and an answer that arrives at minute nine is a different answer. This is also the honest reply to why can’t I use AI in labs: no interview lets you use one either. The lab is not simulating a workplace. It is simulating the gate you have to pass to reach one.

Nothing in Part A is submitted.

A1. The first five minutes

Phone screen, data analyst.

“A CSV lands in your inbox. 40,000 rows, 12 columns, no documentation, and your manager wants a number by Thursday. What do you do in the first five minutes, and why those things?”

Rubric card—interviewer only

A good answer names checks, not tools, and gives a reason for each:

  • row count against the source system—catches a truncated export, which is invisible otherwise;
  • types and ranges per column—catches -999, ages of 200, dates in 1900, and a numeric column that arrived as text;
  • missingness per column, and whether it is patterned;
  • duplicate keys—because a duplicated ID silently multiplies rows on the first join.

The strongest answers add a non-technical one: who can I ask about this file? Provenance beats any amount of inspection.

Listen for the answer that goes straight to head() and a histogram. That is looking at the data, not interrogating it, and it will not catch any of the four failures above.

Follow-up probe if the answer is thin: “You find 12% of the rows have a missing value in one column. Does that change your plan?”

A2. Who is not in this file

Case-study round, data scientist.

“A team surveys its users and reports 85% satisfaction. Before I believe it, what do you want to know?”

Rubric card—interviewer only

The answer has to reach who is missing:

  • the response rate, and who did not respond;
  • that people with strong opinions answer surveys and the indifferent do not;
  • that users who already left are not in the sampling frame at all.

A very good answer signs the bias: both mechanisms push the same way, so 85% is an upper bound. Being able to say which direction without being able to say how much is the mark of someone who has done this.

Listen for “we should get a bigger sample”. A larger biased sample is a more precise wrong number, and this confusion between precision and accuracy is worth surfacing now—it returns in September.

Follow-up probe: “Suppose the response rate was 90%. Does that settle it?”

A3. The join that lost rows

Technical screen, analytics engineer.

“You join customers to orders and end up with fewer rows than you started with. Walk me through what happened and what you do about it.”

Rubric card—interviewer only

A good answer separates mechanism from response.

Mechanism: an inner join dropped unmatched rows, so the keys do not line up—type mismatch, whitespace, case, leading zeros stripped from an ID by a spreadsheet, or the right table genuinely not covering everything.

Response: count rows before and after every join, then check whether the dropped rows are systematic. The sentence that earns the offer: “if the unmatched customers are all from one region, I have just introduced a selection bias into an analysis that had nothing to do with regions.”

Listen for someone who reaches immediately for a left join to “keep the rows”. That converts missing matches into NAs and hides the problem one step further downstream.

Follow-up probe: “You end up with more rows than you started with. Now what?”

A4. Defending a deletion

Hiring manager round, any data role.

“In your last project, did you delete any rows? Convince me it was the right call.”

Rubric card—interviewer only

This is a judgment question wearing a technical one’s clothes. What is being assessed is whether the candidate knows a deletion is a claim.

A good answer states the rule applied, how many rows it removed, and—critically— whether the removed rows differed from the kept ones in any way that matters.

The best answers volunteer the counterfactual: “here is what the number would have been if I had kept them.” Reporting the analysis both ways is always available and almost always more persuasive than an argument.

Listen for “they were outliers so I removed them”. Ask what made them outliers, and whether the definition was chosen before or after seeing the effect on the result.

Follow-up probe: “What would have made you keep them?”

Part B—Build

Roles in this lab

Work in a group of four and rotate the jobs at the checkpoint:

  • Driver types. Nobody else touches the keyboard.
  • Navigator reads the help pages and decides where to go next, and never types.
  • Skeptic owns the self-check cells and answers one question about every result: what would tell us this is wrong? In a four, two of you share this job—which is the right ratio, because it is the one people skip.

At the checkpoint I will pose a question, give you a minute to confer, and call on someone at random to answer for the group. Confer until everyone at your table could answer it.

How Part B works

Every problem has a self-check cell below it. Run it after your answer. It asserts the properties your answer must have; it does not contain the answer, and passing it is necessary but not sufficient—a self-check that passes on nonsense is one you outwitted, not a correct answer.

You are not expected to know R yet. Nothing has been assigned, and the R Primer is what you start after tonight. Each cell names the functions you need in a comment, the self-check tells you which misconception you hit rather than only that you were wrong, and the worked solution is one click away. Use all three. The part of this lab that is actually hard is not the typing.

Cells share one R session, top to bottom. Run the setup cell first, then work in order.

Two halves with a checkpoint between them. If you are not through B3 when the checkpoint is called, say so—that is information, not a confession.

We may not reach B4 to B6 tonight, and that is fine. First days run long: someone’s R will not install, someone will arrive late, the projector will do something. First half

B1. Take inventory before you touch anything

Produce one table with a row per column of clinic giving its type, its number of missing values, and its number of distinct values.

Do not clean anything yet. The point is to know what you have.

```{r}
inventory <- data.frame(
  type    = sapply(clinic, function(x) class(x)[1]),
  missing = sapply(clinic, function(x) sum(is.na(x))),
  unique  = sapply(clinic, function(x) length(unique(x[!is.na(x)])))
)
```

Two things should stop you. site has five distinct values for what is obviously two sites. And patient has fewer unique values than rows, which means the identifier is not identifying.

B2. The identifier that is not one

Return the number of patient values that appear more than once.

Then, before you touch it: decide as a group whether a repeated patient ID is a duplicate row to delete, or a patient with two visits. Your answer determines what you do next, and the data cannot tell you which it is.

```{r}
n_repeated <- sum(table(clinic$patient) > 1)
```

Five. duplicated() would tell you how many rows are repeats; this tells you how many patients are.

The judgment is the point. If these are duplicate exports, drop them. If they are second visits, dropping them removes exactly the patients who came back—the sickest, or the least satisfied—which is a selection bias you introduced yourself.

B3. Five sites that are two

Create clinic$site_clean, a factor with exactly two levels, north and south.

```{r}
clinic$site_clean <- factor(tolower(trimws(clinic$site)))
```

trimws() then tolower(). "North " needs both; doing only one leaves three levels and the error is easy to miss because table() prints them adjacently.

This is the cheapest bug in the whole lab and the one most likely to reach production, because nothing errors—you simply get results for three sites and a sentence about the north that is wrong.

Checkpoint

Stop typing. I will ask one question and call on someone at random.

Confer first: you found four defects in this file. Which one would have been hardest to notice if you had gone straight to computing the average wait time—and which would have done the most damage?

Second half

B4. Sentinels are not data

age contains impossible values. Create clinic$age_clean with those converted to NA, and return nothing else changed.

Decide as a group what counts as impossible before you look at how many rows it removes.

```{r}
clinic$age_clean <- ifelse(clinic$age > 0 & clinic$age < 120, clinic$age, NA)
```

The -999s are a missing-value code from a system that could not store NA; the 201 is a typo or a different unit.

The order matters more than the code. mean(clinic$age) before this step returns a number, prints no warning, and is wrong by several years. Nothing tells you.

B5. What did the cleaning cost

Return the proportion of rows that are complete on age_clean, wait_min and score—a number between 0 and 1.

```{r}
kept <- mean(complete.cases(clinic[, c("age_clean", "wait_min", "score")]))
```

mean() of a logical vector is the proportion of TRUEs, which turns any yes/no question about rows into a percentage in one call.

About one row in seven is gone, and none of it announced itself.

B6. Who did you lose

The last question is the one that matters and it has no single right answer.

Compare the rows you kept with the rows you dropped. Are they alike? Use whatever comparison you think is fair—by site, by age, by wait time.

Then write one sentence, as a comment, that you would be willing to put in a report.

One defensible approach:

```{r}
complete <- complete.cases(clinic[, c("age_clean", "wait_min", "score")])
round(rbind(
  kept    = c(n = sum(complete),  mean_wait = mean(clinic$wait_min[complete], na.rm = TRUE)),
  dropped = c(n = sum(!complete), mean_wait = mean(clinic$wait_min[!complete], na.rm = TRUE))
), 2)
prop.table(table(clinic$site_clean, complete), margin = 1)
```

A sentence that would survive review:

Of 240 records, 203 were complete on age, wait time and satisfaction. The dropped records were not evenly distributed across sites, so results below describe the completed records rather than all visits.

Note what it does not claim. It does not say the missingness was harmless, and it does not quantify a bias it cannot measure. It tells the reader what to be careful about, which is all an honest limitation ever does.
