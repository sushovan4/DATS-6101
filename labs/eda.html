Exploratory analysis on a real dataset, and asking who is missing from it.

How this session works

This is the first session that counts. Last week we ran the build only and demonstrated the interview from the front; tonight both halves run for real. Nothing here is submitted—the score comes from what I see you doing.

Three points, one for each of:

Interview You took both roles in Part A, and your partner signed your rubric card.
Discussion You said something substantive at the checkpoint.
Build You were working on Part B when I came round, and could say where you were stuck.

None of the three asks you to be right. Hand your signed card in before you leave—that card is the Interview point, and there is no other record of it. The two lowest sessions of the term are dropped.

The session opens with question triage, then Part A, spoken and away from the keyboard, in pairs. Eight of us is four pairs, so everyone gets both seats without anyone having to sit out a turn. Part B is at the keyboard in groups of two or three—all of you typing—with a checkpoint in the middle.

No AI assistants. Documentation and these notes are fine.

1. A rule that fires when it shouldn’t

“The text said that a general rule is that if the mean is less than the median then that indicates a left skew, however, in the commute example the histogram doesn’t appear to be left skewed.”

The chapter states the rule as the single most useful diagnostic in this chapter and then, four hundred lines later, builds a variable that breaks it. Both at once:

Where is the left tail? The gap tells you the mean and the median disagree. It does not tell you why. Skew is one reason; two populations is another, and it produces the same gap with no tail anywhere. The chapter has been corrected.

2. What correlation cannot see, and what can

“Can two variables have a covariance close to zero but still have a strong relationship if that relationship is nonlinear?”

“Is there a mathematical way of calculating a non-linear relationship similar to correlation or covariance, or is the best method visual?”

The first has a one-line answer: r = \text{cov}(x,y) / (s_x s_y), and the two standard deviations are positive, so r and the covariance are zero together. They are one question.

The second is the one the chapter had no answer to. Run this:

Spearman correlates the ranks, so it reports 1 for the perfect curved relationship that Pearson undersells at 0.82. Then look at row two. Spearman buys you monotone curves and nothing more. Distance correlation and mutual information go further; none of them tells you the shape.

3. Why n-1, and why variance at all

“I get that we use n−1 because to do so otherwise would systematically understate the variance in the true population, but since when are we calculating the variance in the true population? I thought we were just doing calculations on the data from the sample.”

The chapter defends n-1 given that you are estimating a population. It never argues the “given”, which is exactly what is being asked. If you only want to describe the numbers in front of you, divide by n and you are done—that is the population variance, and it is correct. You are almost never asking that.

“If variance and SD basically say the same thing about spread, why do we use variance at all instead of just always using SD?”

Because variances add and standard deviations do not. Every formula in the rest of this course—standard error, R^2, ANOVA—is additive in the variance and has no clean form in the SD. Compute in variance, report in SD.

4. Two quick ones

“When interpreting IQR/SD, is it assessing for outliers or rather where the bulk of the data is?”

Three different jobs, and only the third is about outliers: the IQR is where the middle half sits; IQR vs SD is tail weight; 1.5 × IQR past a quartile flags individual points.

“When is it acceptable to truncate an axis without exaggerating the difference?”

Not a rule to memorize—a test. Is the reader meant to read a quantity off how long a mark is? Bars yes, so zero. Lines and dot plots no, so cut freely and label the range.

You did not need to have posted to get something here—most of this is a confusion more than one person had. And post early rather than at 11:58: a question posted on Saturday can change what we do in the room; one posted at noon can only be answered in writing.

Part A—Discussion

Format: pair mock interview

One of you is the interviewer and holds the rubric card; the other answers out loud, without typing. Then swap. The rubric cards are collapsed—do not open the one for the question you are answering.

Questions are labeled by role and stage, never by company.

A1. Mean or median

Phone screen, data analyst.

“When would you report a median instead of a mean? And is there a case where you would report neither?”

Rubric card—interviewer only

The first half is standard: skew or outliers, because the mean chases them. Income, response time, revenue per customer.

The second half is the discriminating one. Neither is right for a bimodal distribution, because the center sits where nothing is—between the two humps. Commute times with a car group and a train group; server latencies with a cache hit group and a cache miss group.

The strongest answers volunteer the diagnostic: the gap between mean and median tells you about skew and says nothing at all about bimodality. A symmetric two-humped distribution has mean ≈ median and neither describes anybody.

Listen for a confident “median is more robust, so always use the median.” That is a rule replacing a decision.

Follow-up probe: “How would you find out which situation you are in?”

A2. A correlation of zero

Technical screen, data scientist.

“Two variables have a correlation of 0.02. Are they unrelated?”

Rubric card—interviewer only

A good answer says correlation measures linear association only, and gives a counterexample fast: y = x^2 over a symmetric range has correlation near zero and y is perfectly determined by x.

Better answers add a second failure mode: correlation is a single number over the whole range, so it can be near zero while the relationship is strong and opposite in two subgroups.

The one-word answer to what you do about it: plot it. A correlation without a scatterplot is a number that several completely different pictures produce.

Follow-up probe: “Your scatterplot has 200,000 points and is a solid black blob. Now what?” Transparency, sampling, hexbinning and faceting are all correct. The discriminating move is asking what the plot is for—the first three all destroy outliers, so they answer “what is the bulk doing?” and not “what is unusual?” Worked through in Visualization That Does Not Mislead.

A3. The floor in the data

Case-study round, data scientist.

“You are given a dataset of recorded earthquakes. The smallest magnitude in it is exactly 4.0, and 46 events sit at that value. What does that tell you?”

Rubric card—interviewer only

The answer to reach: that is not a property of earthquakes, it is a property of the recording network. Magnitude-3 events happen constantly; this file contains none because they were not detected or not kept.

A very good answer names the consequence: any statement about “the average earthquake” is really about the average detected earthquake, and the two differ enormously because small events vastly outnumber large ones.

The pile-up at exactly 4.0 is the tell. Real continuous measurements do not stack on a round number—that is a threshold, a rounding rule, or a filter somebody applied upstream.

Listen for “the data starts at 4 because that is where earthquakes start.” It is the same error as concluding that aircraft are never hit in the engines.

Follow-up probe: “How would you check whether there is a second filter you have not noticed?” Scan every column’s boundary for a round number with mass sitting on it—quakes has two more that nobody mentions—and check the shape against what the subject says it must be. The strongest answers reach read the provenance: some filters leave no trace in the data at all. Worked through in Where the Data Came From.

A4. Explaining a distribution upward

Hiring manager round, any data role.

“Describe a distribution to me in one sentence, as if I have no statistics background and have to make a decision from it.”

Rubric card—interviewer only

There is no single right sentence. What is being assessed is whether the candidate leads with shape and spread rather than with a single number.

A good answer sounds like: “most sessions finish in under two seconds, but about one in twenty takes more than ten, and those are the ones users complain about.” Center, spread, and the part that matters for the decision.

A weak answer is “the mean is 3.4 seconds”, which is true, describes almost nobody, and gives the listener nothing to act on.

Listen for whether they check what decision it feeds. The best candidates ask.

Follow-up probe: “Now say it in a way that would be wrong but sound better.” Candidates who can construct the misleading version understand why theirs is not.

Part B—Build

Roles in this lab

Groups of two or three, rotating from last week. Eight of us is four pairs, or three groups if you would rather be a trio. Everyone is at a keyboard. There is no single driver and no waiting for a turn: if you want to try something, try it.

One job still rotates, and it is the one that disappears when everybody is typing. The Skeptic answers, for every result the group is about to accept: what would tell us this is wrong? Hand it on at the checkpoint.

Typing in parallel is not the same as working in parallel. Agree what each of you is trying before you try it, and compare answers instead of racing to one. Three people who quietly write the same thing three times have got less done than one person who was being watched.

The Skeptic has the hardest job today. This dataset looks clean.

How Part B works

Self-check cells assert properties, not answers. Passing is necessary, not sufficient.

Cells share one R session, top to bottom. Two halves with a checkpoint.

quakes is built into R: 1,000 seismic events recorded near Fiji, with latitude, longitude, depth in km, magnitude, and the number of stations that reported each one.

Nothing is missing. No sentinel values, no duplicate keys, no misspelled categories. Last week’s checklist finds nothing wrong with this file.

B1. Describe mag honestly

Return a named vector with the mean, median, standard deviation, minimum and maximum of mag.

Then, as a group, say one sentence about what kind of distribution those five numbers describe. Write it down. You will be asked to revise it.

```{r}
five <- c(mean = mean(quakes$mag), median = median(quakes$mag),
          sd = sd(quakes$mag), min = min(quakes$mag), max = max(quakes$mag))
```

Mean 4.620, median 4.600, SD 0.403, range 4.0 to 6.4.

Nothing here is wrong. Everything here is misleading, and B3 is where you find out why.

B2. A column that lies to summary()

depth has mean 311 and median 247—a mild right skew, on those numbers.

Draw a histogram of depth with enough bins to see what is going on, and then return the number of events within 50 km of the mean depth.

```{r}
near_mean <- sum(abs(quakes$depth - mean(quakes$depth)) <= 50)
```

About 62 of 1,000—six percent.

The histogram shows two clear humps—one shallow, one deep—with a trough between them, and the mean of 311 km sits in the trough. Roughly 417 events are shallower than 200 km and 397 deeper than 400 km.

Now run the chapter’s two numeric skew checks on depth, and compare them against the salary data the chapter calls clearly right-skewed:

mean − median upper quartile gap ÷ lower
salary — “a clear right tail” 0.28 sd 1.85
depth 0.30 sd 2.00

Both checks fire, and both fire harder here than on the example they were taught with. Every number is correctly computed. There is no right tail.

What they are actually detecting is that the deep hump sits further from the middle than the shallow one—which is a real asymmetry, and is not a tail. A tail is a thin stretch of rare values; this is a second population of four hundred events.

The chapter says three independent signs pointing the same way is what confidence should look like. Here two of the three point the same way and are both wrong, because they are not independent—they are two views of the same lopsidedness, and neither can tell a tail from a hump. Only the third sign, the histogram, can. That is the whole argument for plotting before you summarize, and it is why a table of means is the most dangerous object in a report.

B3. Find the floors

Return a named vector giving, for mag and for stations, the minimum value and how many events sit exactly on it.

```{r}
floors <- c(min_mag = min(quakes$mag), at_min_mag = sum(quakes$mag == min(quakes$mag)),
            min_stn = min(quakes$stations), at_min_stn = sum(quakes$stations == min(quakes$stations)))
```

mag stops at 4.0 with 46 events sitting on it. stations stops at 10 with 20 on it.

Continuous measurements do not pile up on round numbers. A stack at exactly the minimum is a threshold, and there are two of them here: an event needed a magnitude of at least 4.0 and at least 10 reporting stations to be in this file at all.

Checkpoint

Stop typing. Confer, then I call on someone at random.

Revise the sentence you wrote in B1. You said something about the average earthquake. What is the correct subject of that sentence now, and roughly how many events do you think are missing from this file?

B4. The relationship that explains the filter

Return the correlation between mag and stations, and plot one against the other.

```{r}
r <- cor(quakes$mag, quakes$stations)
```

0.851.

Read it backwards and it stops being trivia. Big events are detected widely; small events are detected narrowly; events detected by fewer than ten stations were excluded. So the magnitude floor and the station floor are not two independent filters—they are one detection process, showing up in two columns.

B5. Does depth relate to magnitude

Compare mean magnitude across three depth bands: shallow (under 200 km), intermediate (200–400), and deep (over 400).

Return the three means as a named vector.

```{r}
by_band <- tapply(quakes$mag, band, mean)
```

Shallow 4.736, intermediate 4.522, deep 4.545.

A real difference of about a fifth of a magnitude unit. The tempting explanation is geophysical.

The Skeptic’s question first: detection is not equally good at all depths. If deep events are harder to detect, the deep group is missing more of its small events than the shallow group is—which would push the deep mean up, not down. That the deep mean is lower despite that is more interesting than the raw comparison, and you could not have said so without knowing about the filter.

B6. What this file cannot tell you

No single right answer. Write three bullet points, as comments, that you would put in the limitations section of a report using this data.

At least one must name something you can sign—a bias whose direction you can state even though you cannot measure it.

Three that would survive review:

  • The file is truncated at magnitude 4.0 and at 10 reporting stations. Events below either threshold are absent, so every summary describes detected events, not events.
  • The truncation is signed. Small events vastly outnumber large ones, so the mean magnitude here is an overestimate of the mean earthquake by an amount this data cannot measure.
  • Depth is bimodal, so a mean depth of 311 km describes a region where about 6% of events occur. Any analysis using depth should use the bands, not the average.

Note what none of them do: apologize. A limitation is a fact about what the reader may conclude, not an admission that the work was poor.
