Plotting with ggplot2


20 minutes

The other chapters in this part draw with base R—hist, boxplot, plot, barplot—because those functions are always there and every cell runs instantly. They are fine for looking. They are poor for building, and they are not what you will be asked to use in a job.

ggplot2 is the standard in the R world. This chapter is the working minimum: enough to build the figures the rest of the course needs, and one idea—faceting— that turns out to be the answer to the hardest problem in the previous chapter.

This page is slower to load

The cells here install ggplot2 into your browser session, which takes a few seconds the first time. That is why it is one chapter rather than the whole part.

Three things, every time

Every ggplot answers three questions, and the syntax is just those three questions in order.

  1. What data? — ggplot(data = df)
  2. Which column plays which visual role? — aes(x = ..., y = ..., color = ...)
  3. What marks on the page? — + geom_point(), + geom_col(), + geom_histogram()

Read that as a sentence: take d, let hours be horizontal position and score be vertical position, and draw a point for each row.

The aes() call is the part worth slowing down on. It does not set a color or a size—it says which column controls a color or a size. That distinction is the single most common source of confusion for people arriving from base R.

If a legend appears when you did not want one, you set inside aes() when you meant outside. If a legend is missing when you needed one, the reverse.

Layers add

The + builds a stack. Each layer draws on what came before, and they share the mapping declared at the top unless a layer overrides it.

labs() is not decoration. An axis labeled hours tells the reader nothing about units; a figure with no title makes the reader guess your claim. Both are graded in your project.

Faceting is the important idea

facet_wrap() splits one plot into a grid of small plots, one per level of a variable. It costs a single line, and it is the reason this chapter exists.

Recall the trial from Visualization That Does Not Mislead: treatment A beats B among small stones and beats B among large stones, and yet loses when the two are pooled.

Now the same numbers, split:

One line—facet_wrap(~ size)—and the reversal is visible rather than argued.

That is the general lesson, and it is worth more than the syntax: when an aggregate looks surprising, the first move is to break it apart. Simpson’s paradox is not a curiosity that needs detecting by arithmetic. It is what a small multiple shows you immediately, and the reason it goes unnoticed is that nobody drew the small multiple.

Faceting is also how you avoid the dual-axis trap from the previous chapter. Two series on incompatible scales do not need two axes; they need two panels.

Exercise: one panel per department

Draw score against hours as points, in a separate panel for each department. Complete the plot below.

facet_wrap(~ dept)

The tilde marks a formula, which here means “split by this”. facet_wrap lays the panels out in a strip that wraps; facet_grid(rows ~ cols) gives you a true two-way grid when you have two splitting variables.

Note what the panels share: the same x-axis and the same y-axis. That is the default, and it is doing real work—panels on different scales cannot be compared by eye, which defeats the purpose.

Seeing missingness

Where the Data Came From argued that the pattern of missing values matters more than the count. A plot shows the pattern in a way a count never will.

tenure is speckled—missing here and there, no pattern, consistent with MCAR. income is missing in blocks, and the blocks are not random: they are the young respondents. That is the visual signature of MAR, and it is also the good news, because age is a column you have.

A plot that sends you to a number, and a number that confirms the plot. That is the whole method.

Exercise: inside or outside?

You want every point drawn in red, with no legend. One of these is right. Assign its letter, for example answer <- "A".

B.

A and C map color to the string "red" as though it were data. ggplot2 obliges: it treats the string as a variable with one level, assigns that level its default first color—which is not red—and adds a legend explaining the variable you did not have. The plot comes out pink with a redundant key, which is how you recognize this mistake in the wild.

Inside aes() means “a column decides this”. Outside means “I decide this”.

What else you will need

You now have enough for most of the course. Four things are worth knowing exist, to be looked up when needed:

  • scale_* — control axes and color scales. scale_y_continuous(limits =) is how you make the honest choices from the previous chapter deliberate.
  • theme_* — theme_minimal() alone makes a figure look considered.
  • geom_col versus geom_bar — col plots values you already computed; bar counts rows for you. Choosing the wrong one is a common silent error.
  • ggsave() — writes a figure at a size and resolution you specify, rather than whatever your screen happened to be.

Two packages worth installing locally, neither needed here:

  • GGally::ggpairs(df) — every pairwise scatterplot, correlation and marginal distribution in one figure. The standard first look at a new numeric dataset.
  • naniar::vis_miss(df) — the missingness map above, without the reshaping.
Check yourself
  • What is the difference between color = "blue" inside aes() and outside it, and how would you recognize the mistake from the figure alone?
  • Why is facet_wrap a better answer to Simpson’s paradox than recomputing the pooled rate?
  • Why do faceted panels share their axis scales by default, and when would you override that?
  • Your missingness map shows one column missing in blocks aligned with a second column. Which mechanism does that suggest, and what does it let you do?

Where this goes next

Use ggplot2 for your project from here on. The figures in Practice Problems and in the rest of the course are still base R so that they load quickly, but nothing in them is base-specific—every idea about axes, binning, aggregation and honesty transfers directly, and only the syntax changes.

When we reach modeling, geom_smooth(method = "lm") stops being a decoration and becomes the thing under discussion.
