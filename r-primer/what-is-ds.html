What Data Science Is


23 minutes

There is a version of this chapter that lists the tools. R, SQL, Git, a plotting library, a machine-learning library, a cloud thing. It would be easy to write, easy to read, and almost useless, because the tools turn over every few years and none of them is the difficulty.

Here is a better starting point. Data science is the practice of getting evidence to bear on a question, and being honest about how much it bears. Everything else—the cleaning, the plots, the models, the tests—is machinery in service of that sentence. If you finish this course able to write a great deal of R but unable to say what your analysis does not support, you will have learned the easy half.

This chapter sets up the spine of the term. It is not about code, and there is very little of it here. The two live examples exist to make a single point that we will return to in every module: a computation can be perfectly correct and the conclusion drawn from it perfectly wrong.

The question comes before the data

Beginners tend to work in this order: get a dataset, poke at it, find something, write it up. That order feels productive and it is how most bad analysis gets made, for a reason worth understanding.

A dataset of any size contains an enormous number of comparisons you could run. Ten variables give you forty-five pairwise correlations; add a few subgroups and you have hundreds of things to look at. Some of them will look striking purely by chance—that is not a moral failing of the data, it is arithmetic. If you decide what you are looking for after seeing which comparison came out most strikingly, you have used up the evidence to generate the hypothesis and have none left to test it with. You will meet the formal version of this in the hypothesis-testing module in October. The informal version is available to you now: decide what would count as an answer before you look.

So the first move in any project is to write down a question specific enough that you could, in principle, be wrong about it. Next week we will make this precise with the S.M.A.R.T. framing—specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, time-bound. For now, one test does most of the work:

Can you name, in advance, the result that would make you abandon your expectation?

If you cannot, you do not have a question yet; you have a hope. “Does our onboarding email increase 30-day retention?” passes—you can say beforehand what a null result looks like. “Let’s understand our users better” does not. Neither does “show that the redesign worked”, which has already fixed the conclusion and is shopping for support.

The second test is about the data rather than the question: does this dataset contain the thing you are asking about? Transit tap-in records contain times and stations; they do not contain why anyone traveled. Hospital records contain diagnoses that were coded; they do not contain diseases that were never diagnosed. An enormous share of confident wrong analysis comes from asking a dataset about something adjacent to what it recorded, and forgetting the gap.

Turning a Vague Request into a Question

A city transit agency hands you three years of tap-in records and asks you to “look at ridership and see what you find.”

You need to turn that into a question you can actually answer. Which of the following is the best candidate?

Did average weekday ridership on the Red Line fall between 2023 and 2025, and by how much?

A question is answerable when you can name, before touching the data, what evidence would settle it—and what evidence would settle it the other way. Here you can: a comparison of weekday tap-ins on one line across two periods, and a difference with an interval around it.

The others fail for different reasons, and the reasons are worth separating.

Is ridership good? has no measurable subject. “Good” is not a quantity, and no computation can produce it. Before this becomes a data question, somebody has to say good compared to what—last year, the projection, a peer city.

Why do people ride the train? is a question about intentions, and tap-in records contain no intentions. They contain times and stations. You could get at motive with a survey, but you cannot get at it with this dataset, and the most common way analyses go wrong is asking data a question about something it never recorded.

Does the system need more funding? is a policy judgment. Analysis can inform it—ridership trends, cost per rider, what happened after the last fare change—but “need” involves priorities and values that no dataset contains. Handing a decision-maker a number and calling it the answer to this question hides the judgment rather than making it.

Notice that the good question is also the least impressive-sounding one. That is normal. Answerable questions are narrow; the interesting part is what you build out of a stack of them.

Most of the work is cleaning, and it is not clerical

Survey a room of working data scientists on where their time goes and you will hear some version of most of it, on getting the data into shape. The usual figure quoted is 80%. The number is folklore and the exact value does not matter; the shape of it is right, and it surprises people who expected the job to be modeling.

The tempting reaction is to treat cleaning as janitorial—unpleasant preparation before the real work. That reaction is wrong, and it is the source of a great many defective analyses. Cleaning is where the substantive decisions get made, and it looks clerical only because each individual decision is small.

Consider some choices you will genuinely face in S.M.A.R.T. Questions:

  • A column of ages contains three values above 120. Do you drop those rows, set them to missing, or investigate whether 999 was the data-entry code for “declined to answer”? These give three different datasets and can give three different answers.
  • Eight percent of respondents have no income recorded. Do you drop them? If people with high incomes decline more often, dropping them biases every income statistic downward—and the analysis will not warn you, because the arithmetic on what remains is flawless.
  • Two files list the same customers, one as "Smith, Jane" and one as "Jane Smith", and 4% of names fail to match. Whom you fail to match is rarely random.
  • A date column has both 03/04/2025 and 2025-04-03 in it. One of those is April 3rd. The other might be.

None of these has a correct answer that a tool can supply. Each is a judgment about what the data represents and what your question needs, and each has to be recorded so that a reader can see what you decided. This is why a clean analysis is not merely a tidy one: the log of what you removed and why is part of the evidence.

That is also why cleaning cannot be handed off. The person who decides what counts as a valid age is making a claim about the population being described.

Plausible versus defensible

Here is the distinction the course is built around.

A plausible claim is one nothing in your output contradicts. A defensible claim is one you can still hold after someone competent and unsympathetic has tried to knock it down. The gap between the two is where analysis lives or dies, and closing it is a skill—not a personality trait, not carefulness, a skill with specific moves that can be taught.

Two demonstrations. Run them, and predict before you do.

Summaries hide the data

The four datasets below are Anscombe’s quartet, constructed by the statistician Francis Anscombe in 1973 to make one point vividly.

To two decimal places, the four datasets have the same mean in x, the same mean in y, the same standard deviation, and the same correlation. Any report that describes them with those numbers describes them identically.

Now look at them.

Set 1 is a cloud around a line. Set 2 is a curve, and a straight-line summary of it is simply the wrong description. Set 3 is an exact line with one outlier dragging the fit. Set 4 has no relationship at all in ten of its points—x is 8 every time—and the entire correlation is manufactured by a single eleventh point. Remove that one observation and there is nothing left to correlate.

The claim “x and y are correlated at 0.82” is true of all four. The claim “y increases with x” is defensible for the first and indefensible for the fourth, and no summary statistic distinguishes them. This is why Module 1 spends a whole session on visualization before we do any inference: look at the data is not a preliminary, it is a control.

Averages can point the wrong way

The second demonstration is nastier, because here the plot will not save you either.

Read the two tables against each other. Among severe cases, A saves 80\% and B saves 75\%: A wins. Among mild cases, A saves 95\% and B saves 90\%: A wins again. There is no third group. Every patient in the study is in one of those two rows.

And pooled, A saves 81.5\% against B’s 88.9\%. B wins comfortably.

Nothing here is a rounding artifact or a coding error—check the arithmetic yourself, that is what the cell is for. What drives the overall gap is that A was given almost entirely to severe patients (900 of its 1{,}000) and B almost entirely to mild ones (1{,}020 of its 1{,}100), and severe patients do worse under either procedure. A’s overall rate is therefore essentially its severe-case rate, and B’s is essentially its mild-case rate. The pooled comparison is not comparing procedures at all. It is comparing who got them.

This pattern is Simpson’s paradox, and the practical lesson is a question to ask before any two-group comparison: what else differs between these groups? If something does, and that something also affects the outcome, the raw comparison answers a question nobody asked. We will spend real time on this when we get to modeling in November, because a regression is, among other things, a machine for holding such things fixed.

A Reversal in the Subgroups

A hospital compares two surgical procedures on 2,100 patients.

Survived Died Survival rate
Procedure A 815 185 81.5%
Procedure B 978 122 88.9%

Splitting the same patients by how sick they were on admission:

Procedure A Procedure B
Severe cases 720/900 = 80.0% 60/80 = 75.0%
Mild cases 95/100 = 95.0% 918/1020 = 90.0%

Procedure B wins overall, but Procedure A wins among severe cases and among mild cases. Which statement is correct?

Both tables are arithmetically correct. Check them: 720 + 95 = 815 and 900 + 100 = 1000, so Procedure A’s overall rate is 815/1000 = 81.5\%. Nothing is wrong with the arithmetic. This reversal is called Simpson’s paradox, and it is not a paradox so much as a warning about what an average is.

The mechanism: Procedure A was given overwhelmingly to severe cases (900 of its 1,000 patients), and Procedure B overwhelmingly to mild ones (1,020 of its 1,100). Severe cases die more often whichever procedure they get. So A’s overall rate is essentially its severe-case rate (80\%), and B’s is essentially its mild-case rate (90\%). The overall comparison is not comparing procedures; it is comparing patient populations that happen to be labeled by procedure.

Why the other answers are wrong is the useful part.

Small counts are a real concern but not this one—the reversal survives even when every cell is large, and it is not a sampling fluctuation. Nor is subgrouping “cherry-picking”: cherry-picking is choosing a split after seeing which one gives the answer you wanted. Severity is a split you would have written down in advance, because you know before looking that sicker patients do worse.

And using all the data does not make a comparison fair. The overall table uses every patient and is still misleading, because it silently averages over a variable that differs systematically between the groups.

The habit this should build: before comparing two groups, ask what else differs between them. If something does, and that something also affects the outcome, the raw comparison answers a question you did not ask.

The skeptic’s question

Your labs this semester rotate three roles, and one of them—the skeptic—has exactly one job: to ask of every result, what would tell us this is wrong?

That role exists because the question is not natural. When a result agrees with what you expected, the mind stops. When it disagrees, the mind starts hunting for a bug, and often finds one, and often the bug is real—which is precisely what makes the asymmetry dangerous. You debug the results you dislike and accept the results you like, and the process looks like diligence from the inside.

The countermeasure is procedural. Before you believe a number, write down what would have to be true for it to be wrong: a coding error, a subgroup you pooled over, a filter applied earlier than you remember, a sample that is not the population you are describing. Then check the two cheapest of those. It takes five minutes and catches most of what gets caught.

What the analysis does not support

Every project report in this course must contain a section on what your analysis does not support. This is not a humility exercise. It is the part of the report a reader most needs, and the part an inexperienced analyst cannot write, because writing it requires knowing where your evidence stops.

Typical contents:

  • The population. You studied who responded, not who was asked. If 12% responded, your findings describe a group that chose to respond.
  • Association versus cause. Almost everything you compute this semester is an association. Saying “increases”, “drives”, or “causes” claims more than a correlation licenses, and the word usually sneaks in during the write-up rather than the analysis.
  • Precision. A difference of 2.3 percentage points with an interval running from -1.1 to 5.7 is not a difference of 2.3 percentage points. Reporting the point estimate and dropping the interval is the most common way honest analysts mislead.
  • Generalization. Your model was fitted on 2019–2023 in one city. Whether it holds in 2026 in another is a claim about the world that your data cannot address.

A report that says these things is not weaker than one that does not. It is the only kind whose positive claims are worth anything, because a reader can see they were made by somebody who knows what a claim costs.

What AI changes, and what it does not

You are expected to use AI assistants in this course. Not tolerated—expected. You are also expected to catch them when they are wrong, and that expectation is where the grading lives.

Be clear about what has actually changed. A competent model will now write most of the code in this course faster than you can, and more idiomatically than you will by December. Asking for a function that reads a CSV, drops impossible values, and plots a distribution by group is a solved problem. Fighting that fact does not make you a better analyst; it makes you a slower one.

What has not changed is everything the previous six sections were about. A model will happily:

  • answer the question you typed rather than the one you meant;
  • compute a mean over a factor’s integer codes and report it without comment;
  • pool over a subgroup that reverses the conclusion, because nothing in your prompt mentioned severity;
  • drop the missing values, because na.rm = TRUE makes the error go away;
  • write “X increases Y” in the summary paragraph of an analysis that established a correlation;
  • and produce all of the above in clean, well-commented, confident-sounding code.

Every one of those failures produces output that runs. That is the point. The failure mode of the modern toolchain is not the error message; it is the plausible number. Detecting a plausible number requires knowing what the right answer should look like—which is judgment, and judgment is what this course is for.

Hence the AI policy in the syllabus, which is worth re-reading in this light. On homework you submit the prompt log and a critique: what did the assistant get wrong, or what would have gone wrong had you accepted its output unchanged? A submission with correct code and a thoughtless critique scores below one with imperfect code and a sharp critique. That is not a quirk of the grading. It is the grading tracking the skill.

In-class work is unaided, but “unaided” does not mean “from memory”. You may read the R documentation and these notes freely during labs and exams. Function names are meant to be looked up; nobody in professional practice recalls the argument order of merge. Reasoning is not something you look up.

Where this is going

The shape of the semester, in one paragraph. The opening sessions get you set up and get data into shape you can look at, ending with exploratory analysis: describing what is in front of you without misleading anybody, including yourself. The middle of the term is inference—the machinery for saying how much of what you see could be noise, built by simulation first so that you see why it works before you meet a formula. From November it is modeling: linear and logistic models read as statements about evidence, with real attention to when their assumptions fail. The last session carries one analysis from question to written report, which is what your project asks of you.

Underneath all of it is the same question, asked in progressively more technical ways: given this evidence, what am I entitled to say?

Summary
  • Data science is getting evidence to bear on a question and being honest about how much it bears. The tools are downstream of that.
  • Write the question before you look, and specify in advance what result would change your mind.
  • Check that the data actually contains the thing you are asking about.
  • Cleaning is most of the work and is not clerical—each decision is a claim about what the data represents, and must be recorded.
  • Identical summary statistics can describe completely different data (Anscombe); an average over pooled groups can point the opposite way to every subgroup (Simpson).
  • Before believing a result, name what would show it to be wrong, and check the two cheapest possibilities.
  • AI writes the code; you supply the judgment. The dangerous failure is never the error message—it is the plausible number.
