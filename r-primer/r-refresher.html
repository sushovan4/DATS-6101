An R Refresher


51 minutes

This course does not teach programming from zero, but it does assume that the R you met in a previous course or a weekend tutorial has gone slightly stale. This chapter brings it back, and does so with a particular bias: it spends its time on the places where R does something reasonable that you did not expect.

That bias is deliberate. The syntax you can look up—function names are meant to be looked up, in this course and in professional life. What you cannot look up in the moment is whether the number on your screen means what you think it means. Almost every example below runs without complaint. Several of them are wrong.

Predict before you run. Every cell on this page is live. Say what you expect, then press Run.

If you are coming from Python

Three differences account for most of the early pain, and each gets its own section below.

  1. R indexes from 1, and x[0] is not an error—it returns an empty vector.
  2. Almost everything is vectorised, so explicit loops are rare and mismatched lengths do not error; they recycle.
  3. Missing values propagate. NA is not None, and mean of a vector containing NA is NA, not an error and not the mean of the rest.

Everything is a vector

There is no scalar in R. What looks like a single number is a vector of length one, which is why the printed output carries an index in brackets.

The [1] at the start of the output is not part of the value; it is the position of the first element on that line. Build a longer vector with c() (for combine) and the point becomes visible:

Types, and the coercion hierarchy

An atomic vector holds one type only. The four you will meet constantly are:

Type Example class()
logical TRUE, FALSE, NA "logical"
integer 1L, 47L "integer"
double 3.14, 2 "numeric"
character "S", "2019" "character"

When you combine different types, R does not complain. It coerces everything up to the most accommodating type, along a fixed hierarchy:

\text{logical} \;\rightarrow\; \text{integer} \;\rightarrow\; \text{double} \;\rightarrow\; \text{character}

The rule is worth internalising because of what it means in practice: one bad value in a column of numbers turns the whole column into text. A single "n/a", "—", or "see note" in a spreadsheet column of ages is enough, and R will read the column as character without a word of complaint. Then mean(dat$age) errors, or worse, someone converts with as.numeric(), gets NAs, and drops them.

Coercion the other way is deliberate and is where warnings come from:

as.integer(3.99) giving 3 with no warning is a good one to remember. R does not round; it truncates.

Logical vectors coerce to 0 and 1 in arithmetic, which gives a very common and very useful idiom:

mean() of a logical vector is a proportion. You will use this constantly.

Coercion and Silent NAs

You read a column of ages out of a spreadsheet where one cell was typed as "42 " with a stray space and another as "unknown". In R you end up with

ages <- c("31", "42 ", "unknown", "28")
mean(as.numeric(ages))

What happens?

You get NA, plus a warning.

Three separate things are worth pulling apart here.

First, as.numeric does not error on text it cannot parse—it returns NA and emits Warning: NAs introduced by coercion. That is a warning, not an error: the script keeps running, the pipeline keeps going, and if you are knitting a long document the message scrolls past. Warnings in R are the ones that hurt you, precisely because they do not stop anything.

Second, "42 " converts perfectly well. as.numeric tolerates surrounding whitespace, so trailing spaces are not the problem here. The stray space would matter if you had compared strings—"42 " == "42" is FALSE—which is the kind of thing that makes two datasets refuse to join for reasons invisible on screen.

Third, and most important: mean does not ignore missing values by default. One NA anywhere in the vector makes the mean NA. This is a deliberate design choice, and a good one—R refuses to quietly answer a question you did not ask. The mean of “these numbers, and something we do not know” is genuinely unknown.

So the fix is not mean(as.numeric(ages), na.rm = TRUE) typed reflexively. That silently redefines the quantity you are estimating: you are no longer reporting the mean age of your sample but the mean age of the people whose age was recorded. Sometimes those are close. Sometimes the missingness is exactly the point—people decline to state their age non-randomly. Use na.rm = TRUE when you have decided that dropping is defensible, and say in your write-up how many you dropped.

Vectorisation and recycling

Arithmetic in R works element by element, with no loop:

So far so ordinary. Now the part that bites. When the two vectors have different lengths, R does not error. It recycles the shorter one from the beginning until the lengths match:

The shorter vector was reused three times, giving 11 22 31 42 51 62. If the longer length is not a whole multiple of the shorter, you get a warning but still a result:

Recycling is a feature—prices * 1.06 is exactly this rule with a length-one vector—but it is also the mechanism behind a whole family of silent bugs. When the lengths divide evenly, there is no warning at all, and you get a full-length answer built from a mistake.

The dangerous case

length(a) == 6 and length(b) == 2 produces a clean, warning-free, entirely wrong answer if you meant those two values to apply to blocks of three rather than to alternate positions. R cannot tell the difference between the two intentions; only you can.

Exercise: a discount that runs and is wrong

A shop has six items. The first three are on a 10% discount, the last three on 20%. A colleague wrote

prices * c(0.9, 0.8)

which runs, produces six numbers, and is wrong—it alternates the two discounts rather than applying them in blocks of three.

Produce the correct discounted prices, as a vector of six numbers.

prices * rep(c(0.9, 0.8), each = 3)

giving 10.800 7.650 19.800 12.600 24.000 7.992. Writing c(rep(0.9, 3), rep(0.8, 3)) is equally correct and arguably clearer.

The reason the colleague’s version is dangerous is that it never errors. Six items in, six prices out, all plausible. The only way to catch it is to have predicted what the multiplier vector should look like before multiplying by it.

Indexing

R indexes from 1, and square brackets accept four different kinds of subscript.

Three things here differ from Python and cause specific errors.

x[0] is not the first element and is not an error. It returns a zero-length vector, because position zero exists in R only as “select nothing”:

That silence matters. A loop or a function written with Python’s convention will produce empty results rather than crashing, and empty results propagate quietly.

Ranges include both endpoints. 2:4 is 2 3 4, three elements, not two.

A negative index drops rather than counts from the end. heights[-1] is everything but the first element; it is not the last element. To get the last element, use heights[length(heights)], or tail(heights, 1).

Named vectors add a fourth kind of subscript, which is worth preferring wherever it is available because it survives reordering:

The 1:length(x) trap

This one is worth a moment, because it is the most common source of a loop that runs backwards through an empty vector.

1:length(x) on an empty vector gives 1 0, so a loop over it executes twice with meaningless indices. Use seq_along(x) or seq_len(n). This is not pedantry; it is the difference between a filter that returns nothing and a script that produces two rows of garbage.

Exercise: trim the calibration readings

A sensor writes a calibration value at the start of every run and another at the end. Both should be discarded before analysis, and the number of readings between them varies from run to run.

Return the readings with the first and last removed—written so that it still works if the run is longer or shorter.

readings[-c(1, length(readings))]

head(readings[-1], -1) also works. What you should not write is readings[-c(1, 6)], which is right by coincidence: the six is a fact about today’s data, not about the problem.

Missing values

NA means we do not know. It is not zero, not an empty string, and not Python’s None. The consequences follow from that reading and are more consistent than they first appear.

NA propagates

Any arithmetic involving an unknown is unknown:

This is a design choice and a good one. R refuses to quietly answer a question you did not ask. The mean of “4, 7, 2, and something we do not know” is genuinely unknown, and a language that returned 4.33 would be inventing an answer.

You override it explicitly:

But be clear about what that line does. It does not compute the mean of x. It computes the mean of the values of x that were recorded, which is a different quantity, and they coincide only if the missingness is unrelated to the value. If high earners decline to state their income more often, mean(income, na.rm = TRUE) is biased downward, and the arithmetic will not tell you. Report how many you dropped, every time.

== NA is always wrong

Is 4 equal to the unknown value? We do not know, so the answer is NA. Even NA == NA is NA, since two unknowns may or may not be the same thing. Use is.na() whenever you want to ask about missingness.

The same trap appears in filtering, where it is easier to miss:

NA > 3 is NA, and an NA used as an index yields an NA element. The first line hands you 4 NA 7 NA—four elements, of which two are phantoms.

%in% never returns NA

When you are testing membership rather than doing arithmetic, %in% is the safer operator, because it treats NA as an ordinary value to compare rather than as an unknown:

Which one you want depends on the question. == "S" says “is this the South?”, and for a missing region the honest answer is we do not know. %in% "S" says “is this one of the values I listed?”, and for a missing region the answer is a definite no. When you are selecting rows to work on, the second is nearly always what you mean.

Exercise: a filter that does not leak NAs

region has two missing entries. Return a logical vector of length 6 that is TRUE exactly at the positions where the region is "S", and FALSE everywhere else—with no NA anywhere in it.

region %in% "S"

or equivalently !is.na(region) & region == "S".

The reason to prefer %in% is that it cannot silently produce NA, so it cannot silently produce phantom rows when used as an index.

Why sum(x == NA) Is Not 2

Let x <- c(4, NA, 7, NA, 2). A student wants to count the missing values and writes

sum(x == NA)

What does this return, and why?

NA. And notice the useful thing: the code runs. No error, no warning. It hands back a value that looks like it could be an answer.

NA means we do not know. So the comparison 4 == NA is not FALSE—it is NA, because whether 4 equals the unknown value depends on what the unknown value is, and we do not know. That is true at every position, including the missing ones: NA == NA is NA, since two unknowns may or may not be the same thing. So x == NA is NA NA NA NA NA, and sum of that is NA.

The correct tool is is.na(), which asks a different question—is this entry missing?—and always answers TRUE or FALSE:

sum(is.na(x))   # 2

Two habits follow.

Never compare to NA with ==. Any test that could see a missing value needs is.na. The same applies to filtering: x[x > 3] on our vector returns 4 7 NA NA, quietly carrying the missing values along, because NA > 3 is NA and NA used as an index yields NA. If you want only the known values above 3, say x[!is.na(x) & x > 3].

Treat “it ran” as no evidence at all. This is the theme of the whole course. R’s NA propagation is doing you a favor by refusing to invent an answer—but it can only refuse where you asked a question. sum(x == NA) gives an honest NA; the danger is the analyst who sees NA, adds na.rm = TRUE, gets 0, and reports that the data has no missing values.

Exercise: above average, honestly

temps has seven daily readings, two of them missing. Return the readings that are strictly above the mean of the recorded values—and only real readings, no NAs in the answer.

Two separate decisions are hiding in that sentence. Find both before you write anything.

m <- mean(temps, na.rm = TRUE)          # 22.36
temps[!is.na(temps) & temps > m]        # 23.0 25.1 22.4

or in one line, temps[!is.na(temps) & temps > mean(temps, na.rm = TRUE)].

Worth noticing: mean(temps) alone is NA, so the version without na.rm fails loudly. The version without the is.na guard fails quietly, returning five values of which two are phantoms. The quiet failure is the one to fear.

NA, NaN, NULL

Three things that are not each other:

NA is a missing value in a vector. NULL is no value at all, and disappears when combined. You will meet NULL mostly as the value of a list element that does not exist.

Factors

A factor stores a categorical variable. Internally it is an integer vector of codes plus a character vector of levels, and knowing that is enough to explain everything factors do.

Levels are sorted alphabetically by default, and that ordering shows up in every table and every plot. When the natural order is not alphabetical, say so:

Two behaviors cause almost all factor trouble.

as.numeric() on a factor gives you the codes

This is the most dangerous single line in the chapter, because mean(as.numeric(year)) returns 2.8—a small, plausible-looking number—instead of 2019.8. Nothing errors. Nothing warns.

The deeper point is that a number stored as a factor is a symptom. It means something went wrong at import, usually a stray "n/a" in a numeric column. Fix the import; do not patch the column downstream.

Dropping rows does not drop levels

An empty level survives filtering and reappears in your tables and plot legends as a category with nothing in it. droplevels() is the fix, and this is exactly the sort of thing to catch by running table() after a filter rather than assuming.

The Factor That Lies

A survey stores respondents’ ages as a factor, because of how the file was read in:

age <- factor(c(30, 45, 22, 45))
mean(as.numeric(age))

The code runs and prints a number. What number, and is it the mean age?

2.25, and it is not the mean age of anybody.

A factor is stored as an integer vector of codes plus a character vector of levels. The levels here are sorted as text and come out as "22" "30" "45", so the codes are 2 3 1 3. as.numeric hands you the codes—2, 3, 1, 3—whose mean is 2.25. The real mean is (30+45+22+45)/4 = 35.5.

This is the most dangerous bug in the chapter, for one reason: the output is a plausible number. If as.numeric had errored, you would fix it in ten seconds. Instead you get 2.25, which looks like a small, well-behaved quantity, and if the surrounding analysis is about something plausibly on that scale—a Likert score, a rating out of 5—it can travel all the way into a report.

The fix is to go through the labels:

mean(as.numeric(as.character(age)))       # 35.5
mean(as.numeric(levels(age))[age])        # 35.5, and faster on large data

The second form is worth reading carefully: levels(age) is the character vector of labels, as.numeric turns it into numbers, and indexing it by the factor itself uses the integer codes to pick the right label for each observation.

The real lesson is upstream of all this. A number stored as a factor is a symptom that something went wrong when the data was read—usually a stray "n/a", "-", or "unknown" sitting in a numeric column, which forced the whole column to be read as text. Fix the import rather than patching the column afterwards, and check str() on every data frame the moment you read it in.

Exercise: recover the years

year is a factor that should have been a number. Return the mean year as an ordinary number.

If your answer is under 10, you have found the trap rather than avoided it.

mean(as.numeric(as.character(year)))

or mean(as.numeric(levels(year))[year]), which converts each level once rather than each observation.

The failure mode here is not that the wrong answer is hard to spot—2.8 is obviously not a year. It is that in a real analysis the column would be a rating, or a count, or a price, and 2.8 would look entirely reasonable.

Data frames

A data frame is a list of equal-length vectors presented as a table: one column per variable, one row per observation. It is the object almost every analysis in this course operates on.

Note what str() told you: program is chr, not a factor. Since R 4.0 data.frame() and read.csv() leave strings as character by default. Older code and older tutorials assume the opposite, which is worth knowing when you read an example that behaves strangely.

A note on the tidyverse

You have very likely seen dplyr code: students %>% filter(program == "MS") %>% summarize(mean(score, na.rm = TRUE)). It is more readable than the base equivalents above, and we will use it from S.M.A.R.T. Questions onward for real work.

The reason this chapter is base R is that base subsetting is what error messages, help pages, other people’s code, and every AI assistant’s fallback are written in. Being fluent in [ is not nostalgia; it is what lets you debug the tidyverse when it surprises you.

Exercise: a group mean with a hole in it

Return the mean score among students in the "MS" program. One MS student has no recorded score.

Then—this part is not graded, but do it—decide whether the number you produced is the mean score of MS students, or the mean score of MS students whose score was recorded, and whether the difference could matter.

mean(students$score[students$program == "MS"], na.rm = TRUE)

mean(subset(students, program == "MS")$score, na.rm = TRUE) is equivalent, and tapply(students$score, students$program, mean, na.rm = TRUE)[["MS"]] gets there via the grouped summary.

The un-graded half of the question is the one that matters. 87.25 is the mean of four scores, not five. Whether that is fine depends on why the fifth is missing—and why is a question about the world, which no function will answer for you.

Lists

A list can hold anything, of any type, at any length. It is what you get back from most modeling functions.

The [ versus [[ distinction is the one to remember. Single brackets keep the container; double brackets reach inside it. When a piece of code fails with something like “non-numeric argument to binary operator” on a value you are certain is numeric, you have usually got a one-element list where you wanted a number.

Functions

A function in R is an ordinary object. It returns the value of its last expression, so an explicit return() is optional and mostly used for early exits.

Three things worth stating.

Arguments can be matched by position or by name, and named arguments can come in any order. Once past the first argument or two, name them—round(x, digits = 2) is clearer than round(x, 2) and immune to a change in argument order.

Defaults are evaluated inside the function, so a default can refer to another argument. This is how na.rm = FALSE above works.

Assignment inside a function is local. A function cannot change an object in your global environment by assigning to it:

This is a feature. It means a function’s effect is entirely in what it returns, which is what makes functions safe to reuse. (<<- exists and can reach outward. Do not use it in this course.)

Exercise: write a small function

The coefficient of variation is the standard deviation divided by the mean—a measure of spread on a scale you can compare across variables with different units.

Fill in the body so that cv() computes it and ignores missing values. The cell ends with a call, so the exercise is checked on the value that call produces.

cv <- function(x) {
  sd(x, na.rm = TRUE) / mean(x, na.rm = TRUE)
}

giving about 0.455. No return() is needed: the last expression evaluated is the value of the function.

A better version would take na.rm as an argument with a default rather than deciding for the caller—hard-wiring na.rm = TRUE inside a function silently discards data on everyone who uses it.

Control flow, briefly

Explicit loops are much rarer in R than in most languages, because vectorised operations cover most of what a loop would do. When you do need them:

Using if where you meant ifelse is a classic error. In current R, if with a condition of length greater than one is an error; in older versions it silently used only the first element, which is why you will find code in the wild that was quietly wrong for years.

And when a loop is genuinely the clearest thing:

Note seq_along, and note that the result vector was allocated first. Growing a vector inside a loop with c() works but is slow and, more importantly, hides how long the answer is supposed to be.

Summary
  • There are no scalars; everything is a vector, and arithmetic is element-wise.
  • Vectors hold one type. Mixing types coerces silently along logical → integer → double → character. One bad string turns a numeric column into text.
  • Recycling repeats the shorter vector. When lengths divide evenly there is no warning, and a wrong answer comes out full-length.
  • Indexing is 1-based; x[0] returns nothing rather than erroring; ranges include both endpoints; negative indices drop. Use seq_along(x), never 1:length(x).
  • NA means unknown and propagates. == NA is always NA—use is.na(). %in% never returns NA. na.rm = TRUE changes the quantity you are estimating, so report how many you dropped.
  • A factor is codes plus levels. as.numeric() on a factor gives the codes; go through as.character(). Filtering does not drop unused levels.
  • Data frames are lists of equal-length columns. A filter on a column containing NA produces phantom all-NA rows unless you guard it.
  • [ keeps the container, [[ reaches inside.
  • A function returns its last expression; assignment inside it is local.
