Practice Problems


31 minutes

These are not graded and nothing here is auto-checked. They are the raw material for Monday’s reasoning block, where you will argue about them in pairs and one of you will be asked to defend an answer to the room.

How to use this page

Write your prediction down before you run anything. On paper, in a comment, anywhere—but committed, so that you cannot revise it retroactively. The value of these problems is entirely in the gap between what you expected and what happened, and that gap disappears if you press Run first.

Every problem has a collapsed discussion note. Open it after you have predicted and run.

Predict the output

A1. Four ways to count

Predict all five before running. In particular: are the last two the same number, and if not, why not?

Discussion

NA, 2.667, NA, 2, and 3.

The first four behave as advertised: NA is contagious through arithmetic and through sum, and na.rm = TRUE switches that off.

The fifth is the interesting one. x > 2 is FALSE NA TRUE TRUE, and subsetting a vector with a logical vector containing NA does not drop that position—it returns NA for it. So x[x > 2] is NA 3 4, of length three. R’s reasoning is defensible: it does not know whether the missing value exceeds 2, so it will not claim the element belongs and it will not claim it does not.

The practical consequence is that length(d[d$x > 2]) and sum(d$x > 2, na.rm = TRUE) count different things, and if you use them interchangeably—as almost everybody does—one of your row counts is wrong.

A2. The phantom row

Two ways of doing the same thing. Do they return the same data frame?

Discussion

No. d[d$x > 2, ] returns three rows, one of which is entirely NA with a row name of NA. subset(d, x > 2) returns two.

Same mechanism as A1, now with visible consequences: bracket subsetting keeps a phantom row for every NA in the condition, and subset was written to treat NA as “not selected” and drop it.

Neither is wrong. What is wrong is not knowing which one you used. If your analysis filters with [ and then reports nrow(), your denominator includes rows that contain no data at all, and every proportion you compute from it is slightly too small.

A3. Percentages of what?

Five responses. What denominator do those proportions use?

Discussion

Three. table() silently drops NA, so prop.table divides by 3, and you get 0.333 and 0.667.

If you report “67% said yes”, that is 67% of the people who answered, and you have written a sentence about five respondents that is true of three of them. Two out of five is 40%, and which of 67% and 40% is the honest number depends entirely on why those two are missing—which is a question about the study, not about R.

table(v, useNA = "ifany") shows all three categories. Type it every time.

A4. A perfectly reasonable number

What comes out, and what is it the mean of?

Discussion

2.25, which is the mean of 1, 3, 2, 3—the positions of each value in the alphabetically sorted level list "12", "23", "7".

The real mean is 12.25. Nothing about 2.25 announces itself as wrong; it is a plausible number in a plausible range, and if the variable had been something like a satisfaction rating it would look entirely at home in a results table.

as.numeric(as.character(f)) is the fix, and str() after import is how you notice you need it.

A5. ifelse and the third possibility

ifelse takes a condition, a value for TRUE, and a value for FALSE. There are only two branches. What happens to the NA?

Discussion

"low", NA, "high", "high". ifelse propagates NA rather than sending it down either branch, which is the right thing to do and is not what most people assume when they write a line like this to bin a variable.

The trap is the version people actually write:

band <- ifelse(x > 2, "high", "low")
table(band)

which then quietly reports on fewer rows than you have, for the reason in A3. If missing values should be their own category, say so explicitly: band[is.na(x)] <- "unknown".

A6. Sorting

Discussion

"10" "100" "9". Character sorting compares position by position, so "1" beats "9" at the first character and nothing after that matters.

You will meet this whenever an id, a version number, a zip code, or a survey-round label was read as text—which, per Importing Data, is exactly what you asked for when you passed colClasses = c(id = "character"). Both types have a cost. The point is to know which one you have, and to convert deliberately when you need ordering.

This runs and is still wrong

Every cell in this part executes without error. Some print a warning; several do not. In each case, say what the output should have been and what the reader of a report containing this number would be misled about.

B1. Which summaries survive a sentinel?

The mean is absurd on sight. Are the other two safe?

Discussion

The mean is −285.8, which nobody would publish. The max is 74, which is correct—sentinels below the data cannot affect the maximum. The median is 69, and the true median of the four real readings is 70.5.

So the median is wrong, and it is wrong by 1.5 degrees: small, plausible, invisible. That is the pattern worth internalising. The summaries a sentinel destroys loudly are the ones you catch. The ones it nudges are the ones that get published.

This is also the argument for summary() over mean() as your first look at a numeric column: summary prints the minimum, and the minimum is where sentinels live.

B2. Stacking two files

Two months of data, stacked. The mean comes out at 2.5. What is wrong with it?

Discussion

Several things at once, and they compound.

rbind found a numeric v and a character v, coerced the whole column to character, and said nothing. as.numeric then turned "oops" into NA with a warning, and na.rm = TRUE dropped it. The 2.5 you see is the mean of 1.5, 2.5 and 3.5—arithmetically correct, and a mean of three quarters of your data presented as though it were all of it.

The reader is misled about the sample size, and nobody ever asked what "oops" was. It might be a code the vendor uses for “instrument failed”, in which case those readings are missing not at random and dropping them biases the mean.

The habit: after any rbind, run str(); after any as.numeric on a character column, run sum(is.na(new)) - sum(is.na(old)) and look at the values that failed to parse before you throw them away.

B3. A join that made you richer

Every store matched. Nothing was dropped. Total units went from 56 to 66.

Discussion

S1 offers two plans, so it has two rows in prices, so merge produces two rows for it—each carrying units = 10. Summing units on the joined table counts S1’s ten units twice.

This is the mirror image of the dropped-rows failure in Cleaning and Reshaping, and it is harder to spot because the join looks successful: no NAs, no missing stores, every key matched. The only symptom is that the row count grew, and 5 is not an alarming number when you were expecting 4.

The check that catches it is the one from the join checklist: before merging, sum(duplicated(prices$store)). If the key is not unique on the right-hand side, any left-hand quantity you sum afterwards is inflated.

The deeper issue is that the joined table’s row is no longer “a store”. It is “a store-plan”. Once the unit of observation changes, quantities defined per store—like units—stop being summable, and no amount of careful arithmetic on the joined table fixes that.

B4. The average of the averages

A district reports its average score as 70.

Discussion

70 is the average of two school averages. The average student scored 61, because 200 of the 210 students are at the school scoring 60.

Nothing here is a coding error. mean(schools$mean_score) is exactly what it says. The mistake is a mismatch between the unit of observation in the data—one row per school—and the unit in the claim—“the average student”. Aggregated data has this property constantly, and it is the source of a large fraction of misleading statistics in the press.

Ask, of every mean you compute: a mean over what population? If the answer is “over rows of this table”, check that a row is the thing you meant to average over.

B5. The zip code join

Three zips on each side, all three the same places. How many rows come back?

Discussion

One.

merge coerces to a common type, so the numeric 2134 becomes the string "2134", which is not "02134". Only 20052—the one zip with no leading zero—matches. R does not warn, because as far as it is concerned you asked it to match on a key and it matched what it could.

Now look at what survived. Leading-zero zips are concentrated in New England and the mid-Atlantic. The join did not lose a random two-thirds of the data; it lost northeast and kept south. Any regional comparison built on the result is not merely underpowered, it is systematically wrong, and it will look like a finding.

This is the concrete cost of the leading-zero issue from Importing Data, and it is why identifier columns get colClasses = c(zip = "character") on every file in the project, not just the one you happened to think about.

B6. Dates that are not dates

Discussion

sort gives November first and March second, and max claims the latest date is July 15. Both are correct string operations and both are chronological nonsense, for the reason in A6.

sort(as.Date(dates, format = "%m/%d/%Y"))

gives March, July, November. Dates are the single most reliable source of silent errors in real data, partly because of this and partly because 3/1/2026 is March 1st in the United States and January 3rd almost everywhere else—a file that mixes conventions cannot be repaired at all, only detected. (The tell: a day-first file read as month-first has no dates with a first component above 12, which is a check you can actually run.)

Convert dates at import, with an explicit format, and check the range afterwards.

What does this license you to claim?

No code here. For each, decide what the analyst may write in the report, and what they may not. Bring your answer.

C1.

A team joins 4,812 sales rows to a store lookup table with merge, gets 3,104 rows, does not notice, and reports: “Average units per store were 17.5.”

What, precisely, is the claim they are entitled to make instead? Note that “average units per store among stores appearing in both tables” is technically accurate and probably still misleading—why?

C2.

From S.M.A.R.T. Questions: eight commuters, four by transit and four by car, and transit commutes averaged 17.5 minutes longer.

Which of these are supported? (a) In this sample, transit commuters averaged 17.5 more minutes. (b) Transit commuting takes about 17.5 minutes longer. (c) Switching from car to transit would add about 17.5 minutes to your commute. (d) Transit commuters live further from work.

C3.

A housing dataset has 12 rows. Three variables are needed; each is missing for two or three rows; five rows are complete on all three. The team fits a model and reports an R^2 of 0.91.

What has to appear in the report alongside that number, and what would you ask the team at the presentation?

C4.

A team cleans a site column by lower-casing, trimming whitespace, and converting empty strings to NA. They now have two sites where the raw file had five distinct values.

Under what circumstances is that cleaning step wrong? Give a concrete example of a dataset where North and north are genuinely different things.
