Cleaning and Reshaping


41 minutes

This is the longest section of the week and the one most likely to feel like drudgery. I want to reframe it before we start.

Cleaning is not preparation for the analysis. Cleaning is analysis—it is a sequence of decisions about what your data means, each of which changes your answer, and each of which you are responsible for. Deciding that -999 is missing rather than a very low blood pressure is a decision. Deciding that north and North are the same site is a decision. Deciding to keep only the rows that matched in a join is a decision. None of them are announced by R, all of them move your final number, and the ones you make without noticing are the ones that end up in a correction notice.

Throughout this section, one small messy table:

Six rows. Four separate problems.

Three kinds of nothing

The single most useful idea in this section is that “missing” is not one thing in a data file. There are at least three, and R treats them very differently.

NA is R’s actual missing value. It propagates: any arithmetic involving NA is NA, and functions like mean refuse to give an answer unless you tell them what to do about it. That refusal is a feature. It is R declining to make a decision on your behalf.

"", the empty string, is a perfectly ordinary character value that happens to have zero characters in it. It is not missing. is.na("") is FALSE. It counts as a category in table(). It is the fifth-most-common “site” in your study.

A sentinel like -999, -99, 9999, or 1900-01-01 is a real number that somebody’s software wrote to mean “not recorded”, usually because the file format had no way to represent missingness. It is not missing either. It is a number, and R will happily average it.

Zero missing values in bp, according to R, and a mean blood pressure of −246. The first number is a lie you were told by your own code, and the second is what happens when you believe it.

The tool that catches this is summary, because it prints the range:

A minimum of −999 on a blood pressure variable is not subtle. Once you know, you convert the sentinel to a genuine NA and then compute, and the honest version reports how many observations it used:

130.5, over four patients. Not −246 over six. And notice the second number in that output: “mean 130.5” alone is not a result, because it does not say what it is a mean of. na.rm = TRUE silently discards observations, and a report that uses it without saying how many were discarded has hidden something.

na.rm = TRUE is a decision, not a fix

It is not wrong—usually it is what you want—but it changes what your number means. mean(x, na.rm = TRUE) is the average among those we observed, and it equals the average of everyone only if missingness is unrelated to the value. Blood pressures are often missing precisely for the patients who were too unwell to be measured. Whenever you type na.rm = TRUE, ask who is missing and whether they are missing for a reason connected to the thing you are measuring.

Exercise: the honest mean

clinic$bp uses -999 for “not recorded”. Return the mean blood pressure over the real measurements only.

bp <- clinic$bp
bp[bp == -999] <- NA
mean(bp, na.rm = TRUE)

130.5. bp == -999 produces a logical vector, and using a logical vector on the left of <- assigns only to the TRUE positions—one of base R’s most useful idioms.

In a real project you would do this at import instead, with read.csv(f, na.strings = c("NA", "-999")), which handles every column that uses the sentinel at once and leaves a record of the decision at the top of the script where a reader will see it.

"" is not missing

Now the site column.

One NA, in row F. Row C, whose site is "", is reported as present. That is correct—"" is a string—and it is also not what you meant. If you tabulate sites, the empty string gets its own column and quietly becomes a category:

Five distinct non-missing values, plus one real NA, for what is really two sites. north and North differ by a capital and a trailing space; south and SOUTH differ by case; and the empty string sits at the far left of that table masquerading as a site called nothing.

table() hides NA unless you ask

By default, table() drops NA entirely and does not mention that it did. So does prop.table() on its output, which means your percentages are percentages of the non-missing—computed on a different denominator than you think. useNA = "ifany" is worth typing every single time.

The standard repair for text categories is: trim whitespace, unify case, and promote the empties to real NAs.

Two sites and two missing values, which is the truth. trimws removes leading and trailing whitespace; tolower unifies case. Notice the order does not matter here but the combination does—either one alone leaves you with three categories.

Exercise: count the usable sites

How many rows of clinic have a site value that is genuinely usable—not NA, and not blank or whitespace-only?

sum(!is.na(clinic$site) & trimws(clinic$site) != "")

Four. The order of the two conditions matters more than it looks: & in R is not short-circuiting element-wise, so both sides are evaluated for every element, and trimws(NA) gives NA—which is why you need !is.na() in the conjunction rather than relying on it to guard the second test.

Type coercion, and the factor trap

R’s atomic vectors hold one type. When you combine types, R does not complain; it finds the most permissive type that can hold everything and converts silently.

TRUE quietly became the number 1, and then a single string turned the whole thing into text. The hierarchy is logical → integer → double → character, and R always slides to the right, never to the left. A single string anywhere in a column turns the whole column into text. That is the mechanism behind the N/A problem in Importing Data, and it is also what happens when someone types “n/a” or “see note” into one cell of a spreadsheet with 40,000 rows.

The sharper trap involves factors. A factor stores categories as integer codes plus a table of levels. If a numeric column accidentally becomes a factor—which happens with old code, with stringsAsFactors = TRUE, and with several data-reading packages—then converting it back is where people get hurt.

Two things to notice. The levels are sorted as text, so "12" comes before "23" before "7". And the underlying storage is 1, 2, 3—positions in that list. So:

1 3 2 3. Not 12 7 23 7. R has given you the level codes, and it has given them to you without a warning, because from R’s point of view you asked for the numeric representation of a factor and that is what a factor’s numeric representation is.

The values 1 3 2 3 are numbers. They have a mean. They will go into a regression. They will produce coefficients and p-values, and every one of those numbers will be about the alphabetical ordering of your labels.

as.character first recovers the labels, and then as.numeric parses them. This detour is one of the most-repeated pieces of advice in R, and this is why.

Exercise: rescue the numbers

scores is a factor that should have been numeric. Return the sum of the actual values.

sum(as.numeric(as.character(scores)))

  1. The wrong answer, 9, is the dangerous one precisely because it is a perfectly reasonable-looking number. Nothing about it says “this is the sum of four alphabetical positions”.

The habit that prevents this entirely: run str() after import, and if a column you expect to be numeric says Factor, fix it there rather than downstream.

Duplicates

Duplicates come in two flavours and they need different treatment.

Six rows. Order 102 appears twice, identically—almost certainly an export or join artifact. Order 104 also appears twice, but with different amounts, 50 and 55, which is not an artifact; it is a data quality problem, and deleting one of them at random is a decision about which number is true.

duplicated() returns TRUE for every occurrence after the first. On the whole data frame it finds one row (the second 102). On the key alone it finds two, because 104 is repeated as a key even though the rows differ.

unique() on the whole frame removes exact repeats and leaves five rows, which is safe: nothing was lost. Deduplicating on the key is different:

Four rows—and the 104 that survived is the one with amount = 50, purely because it appeared first in the file. Nobody decided that. The file’s row order decided it.

The right move when keys repeat with different data

Do not silently take the first. Find out why. Are these two genuine orders that were assigned the same id? Two versions of one order, of which the later is correct? A join upstream that inflated the table? Each has a different fix, and !duplicated(key) implements none of them—it implements “trust the file’s sort order”, which is a decision you would never make deliberately.

Exercise: how many distinct orders?

Return the number of rows left after keeping only the first row for each order_id.

nrow(orders[!duplicated(orders$order_id), ])

Four. nrow(unique(orders)) gives five instead, and comparing the two is a good diagnostic: if they disagree, some key repeats with conflicting data, and you have a question to answer rather than a row to drop.

Wide and long

The same data can be laid out two ways, and which one you want depends entirely on what you are about to do.

Wide puts one row per unit and one column per time point or measurement:

Long puts one row per observation, with the thing that used to be a column name now a value in its own column:

Same six numbers, six rows instead of three. reshape is base R’s tool for this and its argument names take some getting used to: varying names the columns that are really repeated measurements, v.names is what the stacked values should be called, timevar and times name and fill the new column that records which measurement each row is, and idvar says what identifies a unit. (row.names(long) <- NULL is cosmetic—reshape builds row names like Austin.2024, which are harmless and ugly.)

Going back is the same function with a different direction:

Why the shape matters

Wide is for humans. It is compact, it fits on a slide, and comparing 2024 to 2025 for one city is a glance across a row.

Long is for computers, and specifically for anything that has to treat “year” as a variable. Group by year, plot rate against year, fit a model with year as a predictor—every one of those needs a column called year, and in the wide layout there isn’t one, because the year is hiding in the column names, where no function can reach it.

The tell that you need long format: you find yourself writing the same operation once per column, or naming columns in a formula. In long format:

One call, and it would still be one call with forty years instead of two.

The tidyverse version

You will hear the terms pivot longer and pivot wider, from the tidyr package, which does the same job with much friendlier arguments (pivot_longer(wide, cols = c(y2024, y2025), names_to = "year", values_to = "rate")). We will meet it later in the course. It is genuinely nicer, and it does not do anything reshape cannot—so the concept you are learning here transfers unchanged, which is the point of learning it here.

Exercise: a summary that needs the long shape

long is already built for you. Return the mean rate for each year.

tapply(long$rate, long$year, mean)

2024 gives 3.3 and 2025 gives 3.5. tapply returns a named vector, and those names come from the grouping variable—which is one more reason the year had to be a variable rather than part of a column name.

Joining two tables

Almost every real project needs data from more than one file, and joining them is where rows go to die quietly.

Two rows. You started with four stores and you have two, and R said nothing at all, because merge defaults to an inner join: keep a row only if its key appears in both tables. S3 and S4 have no region; S5 has no sales; all three are gone.

Now the part that should make you uneasy:

Total units fell from 56 to 35. If the next line of your report is “total units sold were 35,000”, you have just published a number that is 37% too small, and nothing in your session told you so.

The other join types are one argument away:

all.x = TRUE is a left join: keep every row of the first table, fill region with NA where there is no match. All four stores survive, and the NAs are visible—they are the point. This is usually what you want when one table is your data and the other is a lookup.

all = TRUE is a full join: every key from either side, five rows, with NA where a side had nothing. S5 appears with NA units, which tells you your region table knows about a store your sales table has never heard of—often a genuine finding about your pipeline.

The other direction: rows multiplying

The opposite failure is less famous and just as damaging. If the key repeats in the second table, rows multiply.

S1 now appears twice, and units = 10 appears twice with it. Sum units on this table and you have counted store S1’s sales once for each price plan it offers. The rule: merge gives you every combination of matching rows, so a key that is unique on the left and duplicated on the right inflates the left table’s numbers.

Keys that do not match for stupid reasons

One row. "S2 " and "S2" are different strings. The same happens with case ("s2"), with a zip code that is "02134" in one file and 2134 in the other, and with an id read as numeric in one file and character in the other. Clean your keys—trimws, tolower, and a deliberate as.character—before you join, not after you notice.

The join checklist

Every single time you join, in three lines:

  1. nrow() of each input, and sum(duplicated(x$key)) on each—if the key is not unique on at least one side, you will inflate.
  2. The join.
  3. nrow() of the output, compared with what you expected. For a left join it should equal the left table’s row count. If it is smaller, something went wrong; if it is larger, the right-hand key was not unique.

Then colSums(is.na(result)) to see how many rows failed to match. Ten seconds, and it converts the most common silent failure in data science into something you can see.

Exercise: count the casualties

How many rows of sales are lost by the default merge with regions?

nrow(sales) - nrow(merge(sales, regions, by = "store"))

Two: S3 and S4. An equivalent and arguably better answer is sum(!sales$store %in% regions$store), which also tells you which ones—and naming the casualties is usually more informative than counting them, because it is how you notice that all the dropped stores are in one region.

complete.cases and the shrinking sample

One last tool, and one last warning about it.

Rows C and F are incomplete—C is missing visits, F is missing site. But look at what complete.cases says about rows C and F’s blood pressure:

Five of six rows are “complete” on bp and visits, even though two of those five have a blood pressure of -999. complete.cases looks for NA and nothing else. It cannot see a sentinel, an empty string, or an impossible value.

That is the shape of this whole section. R’s missingness tools are excellent, and they only work on missingness you have already told R about. Everything before this point—the sentinel conversion, the empty strings, the type fixes—exists so that by the time you reach complete.cases, it is telling you the truth.

Check yourself

A clinic file records blood pressure, and the extraction tool wrote -999 wherever the reading was not taken. You did not know that. Which single check, run before you computed anything, would most likely have caught it?

summary(clinic$bp). It prints the minimum, and a minimum of -999 on a blood pressure variable is not a subtle clue.

The first two options both look for NA, and -999 is not NA—it is an ordinary number that R will happily average. sum(is.na(clinic$bp)) returns zero and you conclude the column is complete. complete.cases agrees with it, for the same reason. nrow tells you how many rows there are, which was never in doubt.

The general lesson is that missingness has to be represented as missing before any missingness tool can see it. Your first pass over a numeric column should always ask whether its range is physically possible: minimum, maximum, and a glance at whether some improbable round number appears far more often than chance allows. -999, -99, 9999, 0, and 1900-01-01 are the usual suspects.

And one more

sales has one row for each of four stores, S1 through S4. regions has three rows, for S1, S2, and S5. You run merge(sales, regions, by = "store"). What do you get, and what does R tell you?

Two rows, and complete silence.

merge defaults to an inner join: a row survives only if its key appears in both tables. S3 and S4 have no region, S5 has no sales, and all three vanish. R does not warn, because from its point of view nothing went wrong—you asked for the intersection and it gave you the intersection.

This is the single most common way a correct-looking analysis quietly becomes false. Half your stores left the building and every summary you compute afterwards is a summary of the survivors. The habit that catches it is boringly simple: record nrow() before the join, record it after, and look at the difference on purpose. If you wanted the unmatched sales rows kept, that is all.x = TRUE; if you wanted everything from both sides, all = TRUE.

A cleaning log

The last thing, and it takes two minutes per project. Keep a list, in your README or at the top of your script, of every decision you made in this section:

  • bp: -999 treated as missing (142 rows affected)
  • site: trimmed, lower-cased; 38 empty strings converted to NA
  • orders: 4 exact duplicate rows removed; 2 ids repeat with conflicting amounts, kept the later timestamp
  • joined to regions on store; 2 of 4 stores unmatched, kept via left join

Every line of that is a judgment call that changed your answer, and a reader who disagrees with one of them can now say so. That is what separates an analysis from an assertion—and in this course it is also a substantial part of what your project report is graded on.
