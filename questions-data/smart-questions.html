S.M.A.R.T. Questions


22 minutes

Every data science project starts with somebody being interested in something. That is a good place to start and a terrible place to stay. “I’m interested in housing affordability” is an interest. It is not a question, because there is nothing you could compute that would settle it, and there is no result that would count as being wrong.

The gap between an interest and a question is where most student projects are won or lost. I want to be blunt about this, because it is not what people expect. When a project in this course goes badly, it is very rarely because the regression was misspecified or the plot used the wrong geometry. It is because in the first week somebody wrote a sentence that sounded like a research question, everybody nodded, and six weeks of work went into an activity that could not have produced an answer no matter how well it was executed.

The five words

S.M.A.R.T. is a checklist borrowed from project management, and like most checklists it is worth roughly what you put into it. Used lazily it is five words you attach to a question you had already decided on. Used properly it is five separate ways of catching yourself.

Specific. Who or what is being described, and over what population? “Do students who attend office hours do better?” leaves open which students, at which institution, in which course, and in which term. Each of those has to be decided before anyone can compute anything, and if you do not decide them, the data will decide for you and you will not notice.

Measurable. Every noun in the question has to correspond to something you can actually put in a column. “Better” is not measurable. “Final course grade out of 100” is. Notice that making a thing measurable is a substantive decision, not a clerical one: choosing final grade as your measure of “doing better” commits you to a particular and arguable claim about what doing better means, and an honest project says so rather than pretending the choice was obvious.

Achievable. Can you get the data, in the time you have, with the access you have? This is where most ambitious questions die, and it is worth separating two different failures. One is practical: the data exists but is behind an IRB, a paywall, or a six-week request queue. The other is deeper: no dataset of that kind could answer the question. If nobody randomly assigned people to use social media, then no amount of survey data will tell you what social media causes, and a question phrased in causal language is unachievable in principle rather than in practice.

Relevant. Would anyone act differently, or believe something different, depending on the answer? A question whose answer is already known, or whose answer nobody would use, is a fine exercise and a poor project. The test I like: write down both possible answers, and check that they are genuinely different worlds.

Time-bound. Two meanings, and both matter. The question needs a time window in it—“in Fall 2026”, “between 2010 and 2020”—because a relationship that holds in one decade need not hold in another, and a question without a window is quietly claiming to hold in all of them. And the project needs a time budget: you have until the proposal deadline, which is roughly five working weeks, most of which will go on things that are not modeling.

Before and after

Checklists are easier to believe once you have watched one do some work. Here are questions students have actually proposed, and what they became.

Example 1

Before. Is Twitter bad for mental health?

After. Among the 1,247 adult respondents to the 2023 wave of the National Health Interview Survey, is self-reported daily social media use (in minutes) associated with the PHQ-2 depression screening score, and how large is the association?

Look at what changed. “Twitter” became a variable that exists in a dataset somebody actually collected. “Bad for mental health” became a specific instrument with a known scale. The population and the year are named. And—this is the part students resist—“is bad for” became “is associated with”, because the data is observational and the honest verb is the weaker one. You have not made the question smaller by doing this. You have made it answerable, which the original was not.

Example 2

Before. We want to predict housing prices.

After. Using the 21,613 King County home sales from May 2014 to May 2015, how much of the variation in sale price is explained by square footage, number of bedrooms, and year built—and does adding location (zip code) improve prediction enough to matter?

The “before” is not a question, it is a genre. Every third project proposal in every data science course in the country is that sentence. The “after” commits to a dataset, a time window, a specific set of predictors, and a comparison that will have an answer. The word “matter” is still soft, and a good proposal would pin it down further: reduce out-of-sample RMSE by more than $15,000, say.

Example 3

Before. Does the minimum wage cause unemployment?

After. In the 12 months after New Jersey raised its minimum wage in April 1992, did fast-food employment in New Jersey change differently from fast-food employment in neighbouring Pennsylvania counties, where the wage was unchanged?

This one is instructive because the “before” is a real and important question that serious economists have argued about for forty years. It fails Achievable for you, this semester—not because it is stupid but because answering it requires a design that lets you say something causal. The “after” narrows to one policy change, one industry, one comparison region, and one window. That is roughly the design of a famous 1994 paper by Card and Krueger, and it is still argued about, which should tell you something about how hard the “before” version is.

Example 4

Before. We will analyze the Titanic dataset and see what we find.

After. Among the 891 passengers in the Kaggle Titanic training set, was passenger class associated with survival after accounting for sex and age, and is the class effect large enough to be visible within each sex separately?

“See what we find” is not a question, and it is the most common thing on a first draft. It is not that exploration is wrong—The Data Science Life Cycle has a whole stage for it—but exploration is what you do, not what you ask. If your proposal contains no sentence with a question mark that names two variables, you do not have a project yet.

The tell-tale signs of a bad question

Over the years the failures repeat, and they are recognizable from the outside.

A question with the word “best” in it usually has no answer: what is the best predictor of income? depends entirely on what you put in the candidate set, and the answer changes when you add a variable. A question with “impact”, “effect”, or “drives” in it is making a causal claim; ask yourself whether anything in the data design earns that verb, and if not, weaken the verb rather than hoping nobody notices. A question that could be answered by a single number with no comparison in it—what is the average salary of data scientists?—is a lookup, not an analysis. And a question you already know the answer to is not worth five weeks, however comfortable it feels.

The most useful move I know is this: write down the table you would need. Not the data you have—the table. One row per what? Which columns? Then go and see whether such a table exists. Half of all bad questions do not survive that exercise, and they die in ten minutes rather than in week four.

Kicking the tires on a question

A question is a claim about what your data contains, and claims can be checked. Before you commit to one, check it against the actual file.

Exercise: is the question achievable?

Count the rows that could actually contribute to the question—Fall 2026 and a non-missing visits value.

sum(roster$term == "2026F" & !is.na(roster$visits))

Two rows. The question is not achievable on this data, and finding that out in ten seconds is worth a great deal more than finding it out in week four. Note that neither condition alone would have told you: five rows are Fall 2026, and six rows have a visit count, and only two are both.

Specific means naming the comparison

The other habit worth building is noticing when a question quietly fails to say what is being compared to what. “Do transit commuters have long commutes?” has no comparison in it, so the natural thing to compute is an average, and an average of one group answers nothing—long compared to what?

Thirty-five minutes. Thirty-five minutes is not an answer to anything, because the question never said what the alternative was. Sharpen it—do transit commuters spend more time commuting than car commuters?—and the quantity you want changes.

Exercise: compute what the sharpened question asks for

Return the difference in mean commute time, transit minus car, as a single number.

m <- tapply(survey$minutes, survey$commute, mean)
unname(m["transit"] - m["car"])

17.5 minutes. tapply splits minutes by commute and applies mean to each piece, returning a named vector; unname strips the label so you are left with a bare number. Note also that 17.5 is a description of these eight people—what it licenses you to say about commuters in general is the subject of the whole Inference module, which opens with Probability.

How this shows up in your grade

The project—a fifth of the course—is proposed long before it is presented. The proposal is due Oct 19 and the presentation is in December, which gives you weeks to discover that the question you wrote is not answerable. The proposal is short, and it is mostly the question.

What I read for, in that order:

  1. Is it a question? Does the document contain a sentence, with a question mark, naming a population, a time window, and at least two variables?
  2. Does the data exist, and have you looked at it? Not “we plan to obtain”—have you loaded it, run str(), and counted the rows that survive the conditions your question imposes? The exercise above is the whole of this check.
  3. What answer would count as no? If you cannot describe a result that would make you say “no, that is not there”, the question is not falsifiable and the project will end in a paragraph of hedging.
  4. Is the verb honest? Causal language needs a design that earns it.

At the presentation, every team member answers questions individually, and one of the questions is always some version of what would have to be true for your conclusion to be wrong? A team that framed its question well finds that question easy. A team that did not spends the whole answer explaining what they meant.

The uncomfortable part

You will be tempted to pick the question that fits the dataset you already downloaded. Sometimes that is fine—it is called secondary analysis and half of published research works that way. It stops being fine when the question drifts silently to fit whatever the data happened to support, and the report is written as though that had been the plan all along. Changing your question is allowed and normal. Changing it without saying so is not.

Check yourself

A team submits this project proposal: “We will study whether social media use affects mental health.” They have one semester and a single public survey dataset. Which revision is the strongest S.M.A.R.T. question?

The second one. It names the population (adult respondents in the 2023 wave), the sample size it is committing to, the two variables that will be measured (self-reported daily screen time, PHQ-2 score), and the quantity that will count as an answer (the size of the association). Someone else could take that sentence and know exactly what to compute.

The first fails Achievable: a survey that observes people rather than assigning them screen time cannot establish cause, so the question is one this data can never answer no matter how well the analysis is done. The third fails Measurable and Specific: “bad” is not a variable, and no row of the dataset is a teenager unless the survey happens to include them. The fourth is not a question at all—it is a plan to go looking, which is a fine thing to do in the exploration stage but leaves you with nothing to be right or wrong about.
