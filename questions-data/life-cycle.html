The Data Science Life Cycle


17 minutes

Textbooks draw the data science process as a row of boxes with arrows between them, left to right, and it looks like a factory line:

question → acquire → clean → explore → model → communicate

That picture is useful in exactly one way: it names the stages, and the stages are real. It is misleading in every other way, because the arrows go both directions and most of them go backwards. What actually happens is that you write a question, go looking for data, discover the data is not what you assumed, rewrite the question, clean what you have, discover during exploration that a whole subgroup is missing, rewrite the question again, model, find the model is answering something slightly different from what you asked, and write up the version of the question you ended with rather than the one you started with.

That is not a description of a project going wrong. That is a description of a project going right. The loop is the method.

The stages, honestly

Question

S.M.A.R.T. Questions. The thing to add here is that the question is the only stage you will revisit deliberately rather than by accident. Every other stage feeds information back into it: acquisition tells you what exists, cleaning tells you how much of it is usable, exploration tells you whether the pattern you were going to test is even plausible.

Acquire

Getting the data. Downloading a CSV is the easy version; the realistic versions involve an API with rate limits, a government portal that hands you 51 separate files with slightly different column names, a PDF that a human was clearly expected to read, or a colleague’s spreadsheet with the units in a merged cell.

Two things to record at this stage, always, and both take one minute: where it came from (URL, date accessed, version) and what one row means. The second sounds trivial until you have a file where you assumed one row was one patient and it turns out to be one patient-visit, at which point every count you have produced is wrong by a factor you cannot recover after the fact.

Clean

Importing Data and Cleaning and Reshaping, and the majority of your time. Missing values, sentinel values, duplicates, inconsistent categories, types that came in wrong, two tables that need joining and disagree about their keys.

Explore

Summaries and pictures, before any modeling. What is the distribution of each variable? Which pairs move together? Is anything impossible—negative ages, prices of zero, a date in 1900? This is exploratory analysis’s subject, and it is also where cleaning problems that survived the cleaning stage announce themselves, which is one of the backward arrows.

Model

Fitting something: a test, a regression, a classifier. This is the part that looks like data science in the recruiting brochure, and it is usually one afternoon. It is also the part an AI assistant can now do for you in about fifteen seconds, which is exactly why the rest of the loop is where your value is.

Communicate

A report, a plot, a presentation, a number in a meeting. The stage where you say what you found and what you did not find, and where the honest sentence about the 78% missing variable goes.

Where the time actually goes

Ask a working data scientist how their week broke down and the modeling is a rounding error. The widely quoted figure is that something like 50–80% of project time goes to finding, importing, and cleaning data. That statistic has been repeated so often it has become a cliché, and the specific number is softer than people pretend, but the shape of it is right and matches what you will experience.

A rough and honest allocation for a five-week course project:

Stage Share of your time What it feels like
Question and re-question 10% Frustrating, and the highest-leverage 10%
Acquire 10% Tedious, occasionally impossible
Clean 40% Tedious, and where the mistakes come from
Explore 20% Enjoyable, and where you learn the most
Model 10% Fast, and the part everyone budgets for
Communicate 10% Always underestimated

If your team’s plan allocates four weeks to modeling and three days to cleaning, the plan is wrong, and it will be wrong in the specific way that produces a presentation on the Sunday night before it is due.

The week-three discovery

Here is the situation this section exists for.

It is week three. Your question is fixed, your teammates have divided the work, and you finally sit down with the file to fit the model. You need three variables: price, sqft, and built. Individually each one looks fine—each is missing for only two or three rows out of twelve. Then you ask how many rows have all three.

Three missing prices, two missing areas, two missing build years, out of twelve rows. Nothing alarming. Now the number that matters:

Five. Not eleven, not nine—five, out of twelve. Missingness in different columns lands on different rows, so it accumulates, and it accumulates faster than anyone’s intuition. On a real file with eight predictors this is how a 50,000-row dataset becomes a 4,000-row dataset without anybody deciding that it should.

Exercise: how bad is the worst single column?

Return the largest number of missing values in any one column of housing.

max(colSums(is.na(housing)))

Three. The gap between this number and the five surviving rows is the whole point: reporting per-column missingness is standard, comforting, and by itself misleading.

Exercise: what fraction of the data survives?

Return the proportion of rows that are complete on price, sqft, and built—a number between 0 and 1.

mean(complete.cases(housing[, c("price", "sqft", "built")]))

Roughly 0.417. mean() of a logical vector is the proportion of TRUEs, because TRUE counts as 1—a small idiom worth keeping, since it turns any yes/no question about rows into a percentage in one call.

This is the number that belongs in your report, next to a sentence about who the seven missing homes were. If they are missing at random, dropping them costs you precision. If they are all the cheap ones, dropping them costs you the truth.

What to do when the data cannot answer the question

You will hit this. Somebody in your team will hit it in week three of Project 1. The variable is not there, or it is there and 80% empty, or it turns out to be self-reported when the question needed it measured, or the years you need are the years the agency stopped publishing.

The instinct is to feel that the project has failed and to start hiding it. Both halves of that instinct are wrong.

It is a finding. “The 2019–2023 county-level data cannot support a comparison of rural and urban outcomes, because rural counties are suppressed for privacy whenever the count is under ten, and 62% of rural counties are suppressed” is a genuine, reportable, useful result. Someone reading it now knows not to spend their own five weeks on the same road. Negative results about data are, if anything, more reusable than negative results about hypotheses.

Revising the question is the process, not an admission. The loop exists precisely so that what you learn in the cleaning stage can rewrite the question stage. What is not allowed is revising silently—presenting the new question as though it had always been the plan, with no mention of what you found. That is where a normal course correction turns into a misrepresentation.

A workable move, in order:

  1. Quantify it. How many rows, exactly? Missing for whom? Is the missingness related to the outcome you care about?
  2. Ask what question the surviving data can answer. Often it is a narrower version: one state instead of fifty, one year instead of a decade, an association instead of an effect.
  3. Check that the narrower question is still Relevant. Sometimes it is not, and you should change datasets. That is allowed too.
  4. Write down both. The report says what you asked, what you found out about the data, and what you asked instead. One paragraph. It costs you nothing and it is the difference between a project that reads as honest and one that reads as lucky.
In this course specifically

A project that reports “we could not answer our original question, here is precisely why, and here is the narrower question we answered instead” can earn full marks. A project that reports a clean result and turns out to have quietly dropped 60% of its rows to get there cannot. Every presentation includes individual questions, and how many rows did you start with and how many did you model on? is one I ask often.

Check yourself

Three weeks into a project you discover that the variable your question depends on is missing for 78% of the rows, and that the rows where it is present are mostly from one large city. Which response is the honest one?

The third. The missingness is itself a result: you now know something true about the data that you did not know three weeks ago, and a reader deserves to know it too. Revising the question in response is the life cycle working as designed, not a failure of planning.

Imputing 78% of a variable means the analysis is mostly reporting your model of the missingness rather than anything the respondents said. Deleting the incomplete rows is sometimes reasonable, but describing what is left as a finding about the full population is false when the survivors are concentrated in one city—that is a claim about that city wearing a national label. Switching datasets silently is not wrong because switching is wrong; it is wrong because the discarded attempt is evidence, and hiding it is hiding evidence.

A note on version control

Because the loop revisits stages, the state of your analysis changes in ways that are hard to reconstruct from memory three weeks later. This is what Git is for, and it is why last week set it up before there was anything to commit. Commit after each stage, with a message that says what changed and why—“drop rows with suppressed rural counts; n falls 4,812 → 1,840” is a useful message, “update” is not. When you write the report, your commit history is the record of what you actually did, and it is much more reliable than anyone’s recollection.
