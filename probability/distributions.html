The Distributions We Actually Use


35 minutes

There are hundreds of named probability distributions. You will use about five.

They earn their place not because they are mathematically convenient—though they are—but because each one is the answer to a kind of question about a process. A named distribution is a compressed description of a mechanism: “independent yes/no trials with a fixed success rate” is a binomial, and once you have said that, every probability about it is already determined.

That is the skill this chapter trains, and it is why “which distribution would you use for X?” is such a common interview question. The bad answer squints at a histogram. The good answer describes the process that generated the data and reads off the distribution that matches.

R’s four-letter alphabet

Before the distributions, the interface. Every distribution in R comes with four functions, distinguished by a single prefix letter. Learn the pattern once and you know it for all of them.

Prefix Gives you Normal Binomial Poisson
d density or mass at a point, f(x) or P(X = x) dnorm dbinom dpois
p cumulative probability, P(X \le x) pnorm pbinom ppois
q quantile: the x with P(X \le x) = p qnorm qbinom qpois
r random draws from the distribution rnorm rbinom rpois

p and q are inverses of each other: p takes a value and returns a probability, q takes a probability and returns a value. Mixing them up is the most common error in this chapter, and the way to catch it is to ask what the units of your answer should be. A cutoff has units of the data; a probability does not.

Every one of these takes the distribution’s parameters as further arguments— pnorm(x, mean, sd), pbinom(q, size, prob), ppois(q, lambda). If you forget them, R silently uses defaults (mean = 0, sd = 1), which is a real source of wrong answers that look fine.

Bernoulli: one yes/no trial

The simplest random variable there is. X = 1 with probability p and X = 0 otherwise.

The generating process: one trial with two outcomes. A coin flip, a click, a conversion, a default, a patient recovering.

Twenty draws with a true rate of 0.3 gave a sample mean of 0.4. Nothing went wrong—that is Probability’s whole theme in one line, and quantifying how far off it is allowed to be is The Standard Error of a Mean’s job.

The Bernoulli is not a curiosity. Every logical vector you have taken the mean of in these notes was a sample of Bernoullis, and every proportion you will ever estimate—conversion rate, click rate, mortality rate, accuracy—is the mean of one.

Binomial: how many successes in n trials

Add up n independent Bernoulli trials that all share the same p, and you have a binomial.

The three conditions, all of which can fail

X \sim \text{Binomial}(n, p) requires:

  1. a fixed number of trials n, decided before you start;
  2. the trials are independent;
  3. the success probability p is the same for every trial.

When someone hands you count data and asks whether it is binomial, these are the three questions. In real data, condition 3 is the one that usually fails, and we will simulate exactly that failure at the end of the chapter.

An email campaign sends 20 messages to each recipient list, and each message has a 30% chance of being opened. How many opens per list?

The parameters mean what they say: n = 20 is how many chances there are, and p = 0.3 is the chance each one comes off. The distribution is centered at np = 6.

Simulation against formula, as always:

0.1925 against 0.1916; 0.2398 against 0.2375. The d and p functions are doing in closed form what 100,000 simulated lists did by brute force.

And q runs it backwards—what open count is the 95th percentile?

Nine. So a list that produced 12 opens is genuinely unusual under this model, and that sentence—this outcome is unusual under this model—is the seed of every hypothesis test you will meet later in the term.

Exercise: a tail probability, and the off-by-one

1 - pbinom(14, size = 20, prob = 0.5)

0.0207. Equivalently pbinom(14, 20, 0.5, lower.tail = FALSE) or sum(dbinom(15:20, 20, 0.5)). The endpoint rule for an integer-valued variable: P(X \ge k) = 1 - P(X \le k-1).

Exercise: simulate it, then check it

mean(opens >= 8)

About 0.229. The exact answer is pbinom(7, 20, 0.3, lower.tail = FALSE) = 0.2277—and note the 7 rather than the 8, the same off-by-one as the last exercise.

Normal: the one everybody has heard of

The Normal (or Gaussian) distribution is continuous, symmetric, and defined by two parameters that are exactly what you would want them to be: \mu the center and \sigma the spread.

The generating process: a quantity built from many small, independent contributions added together. Adult height is genetics plus nutrition plus childhood illness plus a hundred other things, none dominant. Measurement error is the accumulation of many small imperfections. That is the mechanism, and it is why the Normal turns up as often as it does.

The 68–95–99.7 rule

For any Normal distribution, the proportion within 1, 2, and 3 standard deviations of the mean is fixed:

68%, 95%, 99.7%. It does not depend on \mu or \sigma—rescaling a Normal gives another Normal, which is why one table used to serve for all of them.

Worth memorizing because it makes you fast: an observation two SDs out is noticeably unusual, three SDs out is rare. And the multiplier for a 95% interval is not exactly 2 but qnorm(0.975) = 1.96, a number you will type roughly a hundred times between here and December.

The three non-random functions, on a test scored with mean 100 and SD 15:

Read them as four different kinds of question. The density is a curve height and means little on its own. pnorm answers “what fraction score below 115?”—84%. lower.tail = FALSE answers “what fraction above 130?”—2.3%. And qnorm answers “what score puts you in the top 2.5%?”—129.4, which is in points, not in probability.

Exercise: a cutoff, not a probability

qnorm(0.9, mean = 100, sd = 15)

119.2. Equivalently qnorm(0.10, 100, 15, lower.tail = FALSE).

Poisson: counts of events in a window

The generating process: events arriving independently at a constant average rate over a fixed interval of time or space, with no fixed number of trials and no natural upper bound.

Support tickets per hour. Emergency calls per shift. Typos per page. Website visits per minute. Defects per square meter. Earthquakes per decade.

One parameter, \lambda, which is the expected number of events per window.

The Poisson’s fingerprint: mean equals variance

For a Poisson, E[X] = \operatorname{Var}(X) = \lambda. Both come out at about 3.19 here.

This is a genuinely useful diagnostic. Given count data, compute the mean and the variance. If the variance is much larger than the mean—which happens constantly—your counts are overdispersed, the constant-rate assumption is wrong, and a Poisson model will understate your uncertainty. That usually means the rate is not constant: some hours are busier, some pages have more typos, some users click far more than others.

The relationship to the binomial is worth one line: a binomial with huge n and tiny p, holding np = \lambda, converges to a Poisson. That is why the Poisson is sometimes called the law of rare events, and it is why “many chances, each unlikely” and “a steady arrival rate” describe the same data.

Uniform: no value preferred over any other

The generating process: every value in a range is equally likely, and nothing in the mechanism favours any part of it.

Genuinely uniform real-world data is rarer than students expect—“I have no information, so everything is equally likely” is a statement about you, not about the world. Where the uniform earns its keep is as the raw material of simulation: runif is the engine underneath sample(), and under the hood every other r-function is a transformation of uniform draws. You will also meet it later in the term as the distribution of a p-value when the null hypothesis is true, which is a fact worth arriving pre-warned about.

A continuous uniform on [0, 0.5] has density 2 everywhere inside the interval— dunif(0.3, 0, 0.5) returns 2. Densities above 1 are ordinary, as Random Variables insisted.

Exponential: the time until the next event

The Poisson counts how many events fall in a window. The exponential measures the gap between consecutive events. Same process, two questions—if arrivals are Poisson at rate \lambda, the waiting times are Exponential(\lambda).

Two features are worth carrying away. The mean and the standard deviation are both 1/\lambda, so an exponential is always as spread out as it is large—a distribution with a mean of twenty minutes routinely produces gaps of an hour. And it is memoryless: having already waited ten minutes tells you nothing about how much longer you will wait.

The two numbers agree. This is the formal version of the intuition that a bus which is already late is not therefore “due”—and it is the assumption that fails most often in practice, because real machines wear out and real customers give up.

Log-normal: positive, skewed, and everywhere

The Normal arises when many small effects add. When they multiply, you get the log-normal—because the logarithm of a product is a sum, and a sum of many small independent things is Normal.

That covers a great deal of the data you will actually be handed: incomes, revenue per customer, response latency, session durations, file sizes, claim amounts. All are positive, all have a long right tail, and none of them are Normal.

The mean sits well above the median, which is the signature you met in Describing a Single Variable—now with a mechanism attached rather than just a shape.

There is a trap here that catches people who ought to know better. meanlog is the mean of the logarithm, not of the variable:

exp(meanlog) is the median, not the mean; the mean is larger by a factor of e^{\sigma^2/2}. Reporting the wrong one understates an average revenue or latency by a third here, and by more as the spread grows.

The practical move is to work on the log scale—take logs, do the ordinary Normal-theory analysis, and transform back while remembering that the back-transformed center is a median. Nearly every “our average response time is fine but users are complaining” story is a log-normal being summarized by its mean.

Choosing a distribution: ask what made the data

Here is the decision, stated as a set of questions about the process, not about the shape of a histogram:

If the process is… reach for
a single yes/no outcome Bernoulli(p)
a count of successes out of a fixed number of independent trials sharing the same p Binomial(n, p)
a count of events in a fixed window, arriving independently at a steady rate, with no fixed n Poisson(\lambda)
a continuous quantity built from many small independent contributions that add Normal(\mu, \sigma)
a positive quantity built from many small independent contributions that multiply Log-normal(\mu, \sigma)
the waiting time between events arriving at a steady rate Exponential(\lambda)
every value in a range equally likely, by the mechanism Uniform(a, b)
Which distribution, and why

You are modeling the number of emergency calls a dispatch center receives in a given 10-minute window. Calls arrive independently from a large population at a roughly steady average rate, and there is no fixed upper limit on how many could arrive.

Which distribution is the natural starting point, and why?

Choose a distribution by asking what process generated the number, not by looking at the shape of a histogram.

The Poisson description fits this generating story exactly: events, independent of one another, arriving at a constant average rate \lambda over a fixed window, with the count unbounded above. That is what the Poisson distribution is, and its single parameter \lambda is the expected number of calls in the window.

The binomial answer fails on a specific point: a binomial needs a fixed number of trials n, and here there is none. There is no roster of, say, 200 potential callers each of whom flips a coin. (The connection is real, though: a binomial with huge n and tiny p and np = \lambda converges to a Poisson, which is why the Poisson is sometimes described as the law of rare events.)

The Normal answer is a statement about a limit, not about a generating process. For large \lambda the Poisson does become roughly Normal, and using a Normal approximation may be fine for computation—but it is continuous, unbounded below, and will happily assign probability to -3 calls. As a model of the mechanism it is wrong even where it is numerically convenient.

The uniform answer is the one to watch for, because it sounds principled. “No information, so all outcomes equally likely” is a statement about your ignorance, not about the world—and it is factually wrong here: 3 calls in ten minutes is far more likely than 300. Uniform is right when the mechanism genuinely has no preference, such as a random draw from a range, not as a default for not knowing.

When the assumption fails: overdispersion

The binomial’s third condition—the same p for every trial—is the one that breaks in real data, and it breaks quietly.

Twenty ad impressions per user. Suppose you are told the overall click rate is 30% and you model clicks as \text{Binomial}(20, 0.3). But users are not interchangeable: half are casual browsers who click 5% of the time, half are engaged users who click 55%. The overall rate is still exactly 30%.

Identical means—6.00 against 5.99. And a standard deviation of 5.28 against the model’s 2.05, more than two and a half times too small.

One distribution has a single hump; the other has two, and the model’s center sits in the valley between them—the same failure mode as the commute data in Describing a Single Variable, arriving now with a probability model attached.

The practical damage:

The model says 0.7% of users click at most once. The reality is 37%. If you built an alerting rule on “fewer than two clicks is anomalous”, you would have flagged more than a third of your users as anomalies and had no idea why.

The lesson, which is the point of the whole chapter

Every number you would have reported about the average was right. The mean was exactly correct. What was wrong was the spread, and therefore every statement about how surprising an observation is—which is to say, every p-value, every confidence interval, every threshold.

Choosing a distribution is not a formality you perform before the real analysis. It is a claim about the mechanism, and when the claim is wrong the analysis is confidently, quietly wrong in exactly the direction that makes you overconfident.

The diagnostic is cheap: compare the variance you observe with the variance the model implies. For a binomial that is np(1-p) = 4.2; we observed 27.9. For a Poisson it is \lambda. When the observed spread is much larger, some parameter you assumed constant is not.

Fixed n with a shared success probability gives a binomial. A rate over a window with no fixed n gives a Poisson. A measurement built from many small independent contributions gives a Normal. One yes/no trial gives a Bernoulli.

Summary

  • A named distribution is a compressed description of a generating process. Choose one by describing the mechanism, not by looking at a histogram.
  • R gives every distribution four functions: d (density or mass), p (cumulative, P(X \le x)), q (quantile, the inverse of p), and r (random draws). Check the units of your answer to catch p/q confusion.
  • Bernoulli(p): one yes/no trial. Every proportion you estimate is a mean of these.
  • Binomial(n, p): successes in a fixed number of independent trials with a constant p. All three conditions can fail.
  • Normal(\mu, \sigma): a continuous quantity built from many small independent contributions. 68–95–99.7, and qnorm(0.975) = 1.96.
  • Poisson(\lambda): counts of independent events at a steady rate in a fixed window. Its fingerprint is \text{mean} = \text{variance}, and a variance much larger than the mean is evidence the rate is not constant.
  • Exponential(\lambda): the gap between Poisson arrivals. Mean and standard deviation are both 1/\lambda, and it is memoryless—waiting longer does not make the event more due.
  • Log-normal: positive quantities built from effects that multiply—income, revenue, latency, durations. exp(meanlog) is the median, not the mean. Work on the log scale.
  • Uniform(a, b): every value equally likely by the mechanism—not as a default for ignorance. Mostly seen as the raw material of simulation.
  • When a distributional assumption fails, the mean often survives and the spread does not. A mixture of two click rates matched the binomial’s mean exactly and had a standard deviation two and a half times larger, turning a 0.7% event into a 37% one.
