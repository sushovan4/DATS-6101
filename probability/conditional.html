Conditional Probability and Bayes


32 minutes

Almost every question anyone actually asks is conditional.

Not “what is the probability of churn” but “what is the probability of churn given that they contacted support”. Not “what is the probability of default” but “given this credit history”. Not “does the patient have the disease” but “given that the test came back positive”. The unconditional version is a curiosity; the conditional version is the job.

The mechanics are one line long. The reasoning is where careers are made, and this chapter contains the single most-asked probability question in data science interviews, along with the reason most candidates get it wrong.

Conditioning is subsetting

Here is the entire idea, before any notation.

To condition on B is to throw away every outcome where B did not happen, and then compute as usual on what is left. The world shrinks to B, and probabilities are recomputed inside that smaller world.

Two dice again:

Unconditionally, a total of 8 or more happens on about 42% of rolls:

Now restrict to the rolls where the first die came up 6, and ask again:

Of the 16,345 rolls that had a 6 on the first die, 84% totaled 8 or more, against 42% overall. Learning about the first die doubled the probability. That is a conditional probability, and the R for it is mean(A[B]).

The definition, and why it has a denominator

P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}, \qquad P(B) > 0

Read the two pieces. The numerator counts the outcomes where both happened. The denominator counts the outcomes where B happened at all. You are computing a proportion within B, so B becomes the new denominator.

Nearly every conditional-probability mistake is a denominator mistake: someone divides by the wrong population. Whenever you meet a conditional probability in the wild, your first question should be “out of what?”

The multiplication rule

Rearranging the definition gives the multiplication rule:

P(A \cap B) = P(B)\, P(A \mid B)

which is how you build up the probability of a sequence of things happening: the first, times the second given the first, and so on.

When A and B are independent, P(A \mid B) = P(A) and this collapses to P(A)P(B)—the familiar “just multiply”. That collapse is a special case, not the rule.

Draw two cards from a deck, without replacement, and ask for the probability that both are aces:

P(\text{ace}) = 4/52 \approx 0.0769, but once one ace is gone, P(\text{ace} \mid \text{ace}) = 3/51 \approx 0.0588. The correct joint probability is \frac{4}{52} \times \frac{3}{51} \approx 0.00452; treating the draws as independent gives about 0.0059, roughly 31% too high.

Sampling without replacement is the most common place independence quietly fails, and it is worth noticing that it fails less the larger the population is. Drawing two people from a city of a million, you may multiply and nobody will ever know. Drawing two from a team of eight, you may not.

Bayes’ rule

Now the reversal. You know P(B \mid A) and you want P(A \mid B).

These are not the same number and they are not close. P(\text{wet} \mid \text{raining}) is nearly 1; P(\text{raining} \mid \text{wet}) is not, because sprinklers exist. Getting from one to the other requires knowing how common rain and wetness are in the first place—and that requirement is Bayes’ rule.

Write the joint probability two ways using the multiplication rule:

P(A \cap B) = P(B \mid A)P(A) = P(A \mid B)P(B)

and divide:

\boxed{\;P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A)\,P(A)}{P(B)}\;}

The denominator is usually expanded by the law of total probability—B can arrive either with A or without it:

P(B) = P(B \mid A)P(A) + P(B \mid A^c)P(A^c)

That is the whole of Bayes’ rule: two lines of algebra from the definition. Nobody finds the derivation hard. What people find hard is believing the answer.

Which way round is it?

An analyst reports: “80% of the customers who canceled last month had contacted support in the 30 days before canceling.”

The head of product proposes acting on it: flag every customer who contacts support as a churn risk and route them to a retention offer.

What is the problem with that reasoning?

The two conditional probabilities point in opposite directions and have different denominators. The report divides by the cancellers; the proposal needs to divide by the contacters.

Put numbers on it. Suppose there are 50,000 customers, 500 of whom canceled, and 400 of those (80%) had contacted support—the reported figure exactly. Now suppose 20,000 customers contact support in any given month, because that is simply what customers do. Then

P(\text{canceled} \mid \text{contacted}) = \frac{400}{20{,}000} = 2\%,

against a baseline churn rate of 500/50{,}000 = 1\%. Contacting support does double the risk, which is worth knowing—but the retention team would be chasing 20,000 people to reach 400 of them, and 98% of everyone they contact was never going to leave. That is a very different program from the one “80%” suggests.

Change one number and the conclusion changes completely: if only 1,000 customers contact support, then 400/1{,}000 = 40\% and the proposal is excellent. The missing quantity is the base rate of the conditioning event, and it is missing from the report entirely.

This is the same structure as the medical-test problem, and the same structure as the courtroom error of confusing “the probability of this evidence if the defendant is innocent” with “the probability the defendant is innocent given this evidence”. Whenever you read a conditional probability, say out loud what is on each side of the bar before you act on it.

Independence is not the issue here—the whole point is that the two events are related. And sample size is a real concern in general but not the flaw in this argument: with ten years of data the reasoning would be equally wrong.

The medical test problem

This is the centrepiece of the chapter. If you take one thing from Probability, take this.

The setup

A disease affects 5 in 1,000 people—a prevalence of 0.5%.

A screening test is good:

  • Sensitivity 99%. If you have the disease, it comes back positive 99% of the time.
  • Specificity 95%. If you do not have the disease, it comes back negative 95% of the time.

Your test comes back positive. You have no symptoms; you were screened because it was on the routine panel.

How worried should you be?

Write your answer down before you read on. Most people—including most doctors, in studies that have been repeated for forty years—say something in the region of 95%.

We are not going to argue about it. We are going to build the population and count.

Read the table before doing any arithmetic.

  • 514 of the 100,000 simulated people have the disease. The test caught 510 of them and missed 4. It is, as advertised, an excellent test.
  • 99,486 do not have the disease. The test correctly cleared 94,413 of them—and wrongly flagged 5,073.

So 5,583 people got a positive result, and only 510 of them are ill.

About 9%.

Nine percent. Not 95%. A positive result on a 99%-sensitive test for this disease leaves you more than ten times more likely to be healthy than sick.

Why, in one sentence

The healthy group is 200 times larger than the sick group. Its 5% error rate is applied to a vastly bigger population than the test’s 99% success rate is. Five percent of a huge number beats 99% of a tiny one, and it is not close.

The number doing the work here is the one nobody was given prominently: the prevalence, 0.5%. The two impressive-sounding percentages describe the test. The prevalence describes the world, and the world wins.

Now the algebra, and watch it agree

With D = has the disease and + = tests positive:

P(D \mid +) = \frac{P(+ \mid D)\,P(D)}{P(+ \mid D)P(D) + P(+ \mid D^c)P(D^c)} = \frac{0.99 \times 0.005}{0.99 \times 0.005 + 0.05 \times 0.995}

0.0905, against the simulation’s 0.0913. They agree to within simulation noise, which is what should happen—the algebra is a shortcut for the counting, not a separate source of truth.

The same thing in whole people, per 100,000 screened, which is the version to use when explaining this to somebody:

495 true positives against 4,975 false positives. Ten false alarms for every real case, from a test that is right 99% of the time when it matters and 95% of the time when it does not.

The interview question

A disease affects 1 person in 1,000. A screening test is very good: it flags 99% of the people who have the disease, and it correctly clears 99% of the people who do not.

A patient with no symptoms is screened as part of a routine check-up and tests positive. Roughly how likely is it that this patient has the disease?

Think in counts, over 100,000 screened people.

  • 100 of them have the disease. The test flags 99 of those.
  • 99,900 do not. The test wrongly flags 1% of them: 999 people.

So 1,098 people test positive and only 99 of them are ill: 99 / 1098 \approx 0.09, or about 9%.

The reason this feels wrong is that “99% accurate” is a statement about P(\text{positive} \mid \text{disease}), and the patient is asking about P(\text{disease} \mid \text{positive}). Those are different conditional probabilities over different denominators, and Bayes’ rule is the bookkeeping that converts one into the other:

P(D \mid +) = \frac{P(+ \mid D)\,P(D)}{P(+)} = \frac{0.99 \times 0.001}{0.99 \times 0.001 + 0.01 \times 0.999} \approx 0.09.

The healthy group is a thousand times larger than the sick group, so even its 1% error rate produces ten times more positives than the disease itself does. That is base-rate neglect: the prevalence is doing most of the work and it is the number people leave out.

The last option is a real temptation and it is wrong: nothing here depends on the size of the screened population. The counts above are a device for thinking; the answer is a ratio, and 100,000 cancels out.

One thing that changes the answer completely: why the patient was tested. All of this assumes a routine screen of someone drawn from the general population. A patient tested because they had suggestive symptoms is not from that population, their prevalence is far higher than 1 in 1,000, and the same positive result then means something much more serious.

Base rates, and why the intuition fails

The technical name for what just happened is base-rate neglect: when given vivid, specific evidence, people weigh it heavily and quietly discard the background rate.

It is not stupidity, and knowing about it does not cure it. The pull is structural: the test result is about you, and the prevalence is about a faceless population, so the mind treats one as evidence and the other as trivia. But the prevalence is your prior, and Bayes’ rule says the answer is a compromise between the prior and the evidence, weighted by how strong each one is.

Watch how completely the same test changes character as the base rate moves:

The test never changed. Its sensitivity and specificity are properties of the assay and they are fixed. But a positive result means 2% at a prevalence of 1 in 1,000, 9% at 5 in 1,000, 51% at 5%, and 95% at 50%.

The practical consequence, which is not about medicine

A test’s accuracy is not a property you can quote on its own. What a positive result means depends on who you tested.

This is why mass screening of healthy populations is genuinely controversial: at low prevalence the false positives swamp the true ones, and the resulting biopsies, anxiety, and follow-up procedures are real harms borne mostly by healthy people.

And it is why the same argument transfers, unchanged, to everything you will build:

  • Fraud detection. Fraud is rare. A 99%-accurate model flooding an investigations team with false positives is the normal outcome, not a bug.
  • Rare-disease and rare-event models. Same arithmetic.
  • Alerting and monitoring. An alert that fires on a rare condition with a 1% false-positive rate will be ignored within a fortnight, and then it will be ignored on the day it is right.
  • Hiring screens, security screening, content moderation, spam filters. Every one of them is this problem.

When someone reports a classifier as “99% accurate”, the only useful reply is: out of the ones it flagged, how many were real? That number is called precision, and it is the same quantity as P(D \mid +).

The same four numbers, under two sets of names

This is worth pinning down now, because you will meet these quantities again in DATS 6103 and in every interview that touches classification, and the two fields that use them most do not use the same words.

Conditional probability Medicine and screening Machine learning
P(+ \mid D) sensitivity recall, true positive rate
P(- \mid \text{no } D) specificity true negative rate
P(D \mid +) positive predictive value precision
P(\text{no } D \mid -) negative predictive value —

They are the same four numbers. Read the table by which side of the bar the test result sits on: sensitivity and recall condition on the truth and describe the test, so they do not move when the disease becomes rarer. Precision and PPV condition on the result and describe what a flag is worth, so they collapse when the base rate falls—which is the whole content of the previous section.

That is also why a model can be reported as having 99% recall and be nearly useless. Recall is a property of the classifier; precision is a property of the classifier and the population you deployed it into, and only one of those is in the paper.

The other conditional, for completeness

A negative result, on the other hand, is enormously reassuring—precisely because the disease is rare:

About 4 in 100,000. The same low prevalence that made the positive result nearly meaningless makes the negative result almost conclusive. Both facts come from the same base rate, and if only one of them surprises you, that is worth sitting with.

Exercises

Exercise: the conditional, from the simulated population

mean(disease[positive == 1])

About 0.091. Equivalently mean(disease & positive) / mean(positive), or sum(disease == 1 & positive == 1) / sum(positive == 1).

Exercise: the same table, the other way round

mean(positive[disease == 1])

About 0.992. This is the number the test manufacturer advertises. The previous exercise is the number the patient cares about.

Exercise: Bayes without the simulation

sens * prev / (sens * prev + (1 - spec) * (1 - prev))

0.0905.

Exercise: the same test, a different clinic

sens * 0.05 / (sens * 0.05 + (1 - spec) * 0.95)

0.510. Ten times the prevalence turns a 9% posterior into a 51% one. This is also the honest answer to “but surely my doctor ordered the test for a reason”—if you were tested because of symptoms, you are not in the 0.5% population any more.

Exercise: what do you tell the patient?

answer <- "B"

The full picture needs both numbers: the posterior (9%) and the update (0.5% to 9%, an eighteen-fold increase). Reporting only the first makes the test sound useless; reporting only the second makes it sound damning.

Summary

  • Conditioning is subsetting. P(A \mid B) is computed by discarding everything outside B and counting what remains: mean(a[b == 1]).
  • P(A \mid B) = P(A \cap B) / P(B). Most errors are denominator errors, so always ask “out of what?”
  • Multiplication rule: P(A \cap B) = P(B)P(A \mid B). Plain multiplication is the independent special case, and sampling without replacement is not it.
  • Bayes’ rule reverses a conditional, and it needs the base rate to do so. P(A \mid B) \ne P(B \mid A), and they can differ by an order of magnitude.
  • A 99%-sensitive, 95%-specific test for a disease with 0.5% prevalence gives a positive predictive value of 9%. That is not a paradox; it is what happens when a small error rate is applied to a much larger group.
  • Base-rate neglect is the habit of weighing the vivid evidence and forgetting the prior. It is why “99% accurate” is not a reportable property of a classifier and why “precision” exists as a separate word.
  • The same arithmetic governs fraud detection, alerting, screening, and every rare-event classifier you will ever build.
