Probability


29 minutes

Everything in the last three weeks was a statement about data you had. From here on, every statement is about data you might have had, and probability is the vocabulary for that.

The awkward thing about probability is that it is easy to compute and hard to believe. The algebra fits on a postcard. The mistakes are almost never algebraic: they come from writing down the wrong model of the situation, confidently, and then doing correct arithmetic on it. That is why this course does simulation first. A simulation forces you to state the process—what gets flipped, what gets drawn, what depends on what—and then it counts, without any opinion about what the answer ought to be.

Outcomes, events, and probability

Three words, used precisely from here on.

An outcome is one complete result of a random process. Roll two dice and the outcome is a pair, like (3, 4). There are 36 of them, and the collection of all possible outcomes is the sample space.

An event is a set of outcomes—something you can ask a yes/no question about. “The dice sum to 7” is an event containing six outcomes. “At least one die shows a 6” is an event containing eleven. Events are the things probability assigns numbers to.

A probability is a number between 0 and 1 attached to an event, obeying three rules that are less a discovery than a decision about what the word should mean:

  1. P(A) \ge 0 for every event.
  2. P(\text{something happens}) = 1.
  3. If A and B cannot both occur, P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B).

Everything else in this chapter follows from those three lines.

What the number means

There are two respectable answers, and it is worth knowing that the argument exists rather than being ambushed by it later.

The frequentist reading: P(A) is the proportion of times A happens if you repeat the process forever. A probability is a property of a repeatable experiment.

The Bayesian reading: P(A) is a degree of belief, which you may hold about things that happen only once—“the probability this model ships on time”.

This course is mostly frequentist in its machinery, because confidence intervals and p-values are frequentist objects and you need to be able to say precisely what they claim. But the frequentist reading has a real cost: it makes “probability” meaningless for one-off events, and people quietly use it that way constantly. Notice when you are doing it.

The useful thing about the frequentist reading is that it is literally what a simulation does. So let us watch it happen.

By a thousand flips it is 0.498. By a hundred thousand, 0.5004.

Plot the running proportion, on a log scale so that the early chaos and the late calm are both visible:

What that picture is and is not saying

The curve settles toward 0.5. That is the law of large numbers, and it is the whole justification for using simulation as a tool.

What it does not say is that the coin “corrects itself”. After a run of ten heads, the next flip is still 50/50—the coin has no memory and no obligation. The proportion converges because the early results get diluted by an ever-growing denominator, not because later flips compensate. Nothing pushes back; the imbalance just stops mattering.

That distinction is the gambler’s fallacy, and it survives in professional settings dressed as “we’re due for a good quarter”.

The same picture is also a warning about sample size. At n = 10 the estimate was off by 0.30. At n = 1{,}000 it was off by 0.002. How fast that error shrinks is exactly the question The Standard Error of a Mean answers, and the answer—1/\sqrt{n}—is the single most load-bearing fact in the rest of this course.

Events, and combining them

Take a concrete process: roll two fair dice, 100,000 times.

Notice what seven and six are: logical vectors, one entry per roll, TRUE where the event occurred. An event really is a subset of the outcomes, and in R a subset is a logical vector. The probability of an event is then the mean of that vector, because mean() of TRUE/FALSE counts the proportion of TRUEs—the same trick you used in Describing a Single Variable.

About 0.165 and 0.301. The exact answers are 6/36 = 0.1667 and 11/36 = 0.3056, and the simulation lands close to both.

The complement rule

The event “not A” is the complement A^c, and

P(A^c) = 1 - P(A).

This is not a triviality—it is the single most useful computational trick in elementary probability, because “at least one” is usually painful to count directly and “none” is usually easy.

Same number, which it must be. The payoff shows up when the direct count is hard: the probability of at least one 6 in ten rolls is a nuisance to enumerate, but the probability of no 6 is (5/6)^{10}, so the answer is 1 - (5/6)^{10}.

The addition rule

For any two events,

P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B).

You subtract the overlap because adding the two counts it twice. When A and B are mutually exclusive—they cannot both happen—the overlap is empty and the rule collapses to P(A) + P(B), which is axiom 3.

Check it against the simulation:

Simply adding gives 0.466. The correct answer is 0.411. The gap is the 5.5% of rolls that are both a seven and a six—(1,6) and (6,1)—counted twice.

Two dice, two events, one of the two most basic rules in the subject, and naive addition is off by 13%. This is the whole argument for checking yourself by counting.

Independence versus mutual exclusivity

Now the section that this chapter exists for.

These two terms describe opposite situations, and they are confused so routinely that “are mutually exclusive events independent?” is a standard screening question in data science interviews. It is asked because the wrong answer is fast and confident.

The two definitions, side by side

Mutually exclusive (disjoint): A and B cannot both happen. P(A \cap B) = 0

Independent: knowing whether B happened tells you nothing about A. P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B), \qquad \text{equivalently } P(A \mid B) = P(A)

These are not variations on a theme. If A and B are mutually exclusive and both have positive probability, they are maximally dependent: the moment you learn B occurred, P(A) drops to zero. Knowing B told you everything.

Two mutually exclusive events can be independent only if one of them has probability zero, which is a degenerate case with nothing in it.

Confirm it on the dice. “Sums to 7” and “sums to 10” are mutually exclusive:

P(A \cap B) = 0 but P(A)P(B) \approx 0.0136. Zero is not 0.0136, so the events are not independent—they are as dependent as two events get.

Meanwhile, “the first die is a 6” and “the second die is a 6” are independent, because the dice do not communicate:

0.0282 against 0.0270, with the exact answer 1/36 = 0.0278 sitting between them. The joint probability and the product of the marginals agree to within simulation noise, and that agreement is what independence looks like when you check it by counting rather than by asserting it.

Exclusive or independent

A single card is drawn from a well-shuffled deck. Let A be “the card is a heart” and B be “the card is a spade”.

Which statement is correct?

This is the confusion the chapter is built around, and it is worth stating in one line: mutual exclusivity is the strongest possible form of dependence.

Independence means P(A \mid B) = P(A)—knowing B tells you nothing about A. Here, knowing the card is a spade tells you everything about whether it is a heart: it is not. P(A \mid B) = 0 while P(A) = 1/4, so the two events are about as far from independent as events can get.

The arithmetic says the same thing. Independence requires P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B). For these events P(A \cap B) = 0 but P(A)P(B) = 1/4 \times 1/4 = 1/16. Zero does not equal 1/16, so they are not independent. The only way two mutually exclusive events can also be independent is if at least one of them has probability zero—a degenerate case with nothing in it.

The first option confuses “one physical action” with “no informational link”; independence is about information, not about causal influence. The third option is the specific error of thinking P(A \cap B) = 0 is the independence condition, when the condition is a product. The fourth invents a restriction that does not exist: independence is a statement about a joint probability, and a single draw has one.

When independence is assumed and is not true

Here is why this matters outside a quiz.

Two servers sit in the same rack. Each fails on about 5% of days. You are asked: what is the probability that both are down at once, taking the service offline?

The instant answer is 0.05 \times 0.05 = 0.0025, one day in 400. That answer assumes independence. Nobody stated independence; it got assumed because the multiplication was available.

But the servers share a rack, and the rack shares a power supply. Model it honestly: on 2% of days the rack power fails and both servers go down together. On the other days, each fails on its own with a small probability, tuned so that each server’s overall failure rate is still exactly the 5% you were told.

Each server is down about 5% of the time, exactly as reported. The marginal numbers are not in dispute. Now the joint:

The real answer is about 0.0205, roughly one day in 49. The independence answer was 0.0025, one day in 400. The assumption was wrong by a factor of eight, and every marginal number that went into it was correct.

Look at it as a conditional and the mechanism is obvious:

Given that server B is down, server A is down 41% of the time—not 5%. B being down is strong evidence that the rack lost power, and the rack losing power takes A with it.

The general shape of this error

Independence fails whenever two things share a hidden common cause. The pattern recurs everywhere:

  • Two servers in one rack, one data center, one cloud region.
  • Two loans that default independently—until a recession arrives, which is precisely what happened in 2008 to models that had priced mortgage pools assuming independence.
  • Two “independent” A/B test users who are actually the same person on two devices.
  • Repeated measurements on the same patient, treated as separate observations.

Multiplying probabilities is the most-used move in applied probability and the most-abused. Before you multiply, say out loud: what would have to be true for these to be independent, and is it?

You will meet this again in The Standard Error of a Mean in a friendlier form. Variances add for independent quantities—which is the fact that makes \operatorname{Var}(\bar X) = \sigma^2/n work—and if the observations are not independent, that formula is wrong too, in the same direction and for the same reason.

Summary

  • An outcome is one result; an event is a set of outcomes; a probability is a number on an event obeying three short rules. In R, an event is a logical vector and its probability is mean() of that vector.
  • The frequentist reading of a probability is a long-run frequency, and that is exactly what a simulation computes. Ten flips told us 0.2; a hundred thousand told us 0.5004.
  • The proportion settles because early results get diluted, not because later results compensate. Nothing is ever “due”.
  • Complement rule: P(A^c) = 1 - P(A). Use it whenever the phrase “at least one” appears.
  • Addition rule: P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B). Forgetting the overlap overstated a two-dice answer by 13%.
  • Mutually exclusive means P(A \cap B) = 0. Independent means P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B). Events that are mutually exclusive and have positive probability are the most dependent events there are.
  • Assuming independence because multiplication was convenient made a correlated-failure answer wrong by a factor of eight, with every input correct.
