Practice Problems


26 minutes

None of these are graded and none of them are auto-checked. They are here so you arrive on Monday with something to argue about.

Work them in order. Most of the first section comes in two parts: predict, then run. The prediction is where the learning happens. Probability is the topic where skipping straight to the answer is most tempting and most useless, because every output looks reasonable once you have seen it.

Write your prediction down. On paper, in a comment, in a note on your phone. Then run it.

What to bring to class

For each problem, bring an answer you are prepared to be wrong about. In probability, being wrong is the normal case and it is how the intuition gets built—several of these are designed so that the confident answer is the wrong one.

The in-class discussion is graded on whether you said something substantive and could defend it, not on whether it was correct.

Predict before you run

A1. Twenty flips

Predict all four numbers before running.

Then answer: the fair coin came up 1 sixteen times out of twenty. Is this evidence that sample() is broken? Write down what you would need to compute to decide, without computing it—you will have the tools in three weeks. Then say what the longest_run figure would have to be before you started to worry, and why “that looks non-random” is a bad instinct.

A2. Two events on one die

Predict all four numbers, and in particular predict whether the last two will be equal.

Then answer: A and B are events on a single roll of a single die, and they are independent. Explain how that is possible when they are computed from the same number. Then construct a third event C on the same die that is not independent of A, and say how you would know before simulating.

A3. The screen, at a different prevalence

Do not run this yet. Write down your guess for the second number first.

Then answer: state, in one sentence each, which of the two numbers a test manufacturer would print on the box, which one a patient is asking for, and which one a hospital deciding whether to run the screen at all should care about. They are not all the same.

A4. The Normal, checked

Predict all four before running.

Then answer: the last two numbers do not match exactly. Which one is “right”? Say precisely what each of them estimates, and which you would report if these 100,000 values were real measurements rather than draws from a distribution you chose yourself.

A5. Same mean, different world

Predict which pair of numbers will match and which will not.

Then answer: the variances differ by a factor of about seven. Name a business decision that would be identical under both models and one that would be badly wrong under the first. Then say which single plot would have revealed the difference in two seconds.

This runs, and it answers a different question

Every cell below executes cleanly and prints a correct number. Each one is attached to a stated task that it does not answer. Find the mismatch, say what question the code actually answers, and write the code that answers the one asked.

This is the harder skill and the more valuable one. Code that crashes tells you it is broken.

B1. At least one six

Task: you roll four dice. What is the probability of getting at least one six?

Hint, once you have a guess

What is mean(sixes == 2)? Should that outcome count toward the task’s question?

Fix it, then compute the exact answer with the complement rule and check that the two agree.

B2. The reassuring number

Task: a patient tests positive on the screen in problem A3. Report the probability that they have the disease.

The number printed is correct and it is not the answer. Say which conditional probability it is, compute the one that was asked for, and then write the sentence you would actually say to the patient—including both the posterior probability and the size of the update from the prior.

B3. Two flights

Task: you have a connection with a two-hour layover. Flight A and flight B each run late about 20% of the time. What is the probability that both legs are late?

Every marginal probability here is correct. The final line is not. Compute the right answer from the same three vectors, state the ratio between the two, and name the variable in the code that is the reason. Then give two more examples from your own field where the same shared-cause structure would break a multiplication.

B4. Two children

Task: a family has two children. You learn that at least one of them is a girl. What is the probability that both are girls?

The answer printed is about 0.5. The answer to the task is not 0.5.

Find the line that conditions on the wrong event, fix it, and explain the gap in terms of denominators: how many families are in each of the two conditioning sets, and which families are in one but not the other. This is a famous problem and the argument about it is usually about the wording, not the arithmetic—so also state one plausible way of learning “at least one is a girl” that would genuinely make 0.5 the right answer.

B5. The alert threshold

Task: an error-logging service sees about 7 errors an hour. Set an alert threshold so that it fires on roughly 1% of normal hours.

The threshold was computed correctly from a Poisson with the correct mean. The alert fires on 11% of hours, not 1%.

Diagnose it using a comparison the chapter told you to run on every count variable. Then propose a fix, and say what the fix costs—there is more than one defensible answer here, and “use the empirical quantile instead” is only one of them.

B6. The classifier that is 97% accurate

Task: a fraud model flags transactions. Report whether it is any good.

Accuracy of 0.97 sounds excellent. Compute two further numbers from the same vectors: the proportion of flagged transactions that are actually fraud, and the proportion of fraud that gets flagged. Then compute the accuracy of the model that flags nothing at all, and say what that comparison tells you about accuracy as a metric for rare events.

Judgment

No code. Bring written answers; we will disagree about these out loud.

C1.

You are asked in an interview: “Are mutually exclusive events independent?”

Write the answer you would give out loud, in under thirty seconds, including one concrete example. Then write the follow-up question you would expect next, and answer that too.

C2.

A public health agency is deciding whether to screen the whole adult population for a disease with a prevalence of 1 in 2,000, using a test with 99% sensitivity and 98% specificity.

Make the strongest case for the program and the strongest case against it, each in three sentences, using numbers you compute yourself. Then say which single additional piece of information would settle it for you.

C3.

Your A/B test assigns visitors to a variant and records whether they convert. The analysis treats the visitors as independent.

List three concrete ways that assumption can fail in a real product, and for each one say whether it makes your reported uncertainty too small, too large, or unclear. (You will be able to prove the direction after The Standard Error of a Mean; for now, argue it.)

C4.

Conditional Probability and Bayes showed that a positive result on an excellent test can leave you at 9% probability of illness, and also that the same result raises your risk eighteenfold.

Both statements are true of the same number. Write a short paragraph—the kind you would put in a report—that conveys both without leaving the reader with a false impression in either direction. Then write the version a journalist would print from it, and say what you would do differently to prevent that.

C5.

Everything this week was about a process you chose: you knew the true probability, so you could check the answer.

Real data does not come with the true probability attached. Pick any one quantity from these chapters—the 9% posterior, the 0.30 chance of at least one six, the click rate of 0.3—and answer: if you only had the data and not the generating process, how would you know your distributional assumption was wrong?

Bring your list of checks to class. Some of them are standard-error and hypothesis-testing material, and I would rather you wrote them down first.
