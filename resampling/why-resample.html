Why Resample


32 minutes

Here is the honest position you are in after eleven weeks.

You can put a give-or-take on a mean. You can put one on a difference of two means, which is a mean in disguise. You can put one on a proportion, which is the mean of a column of zeros and ones. And in the modeling weeks you get standard errors for regression coefficients, which are weighted means of the outcome.

That is the list.

It is not a coincidence that the list is short. Every item on it is a mean, because the machinery of The Standard Error of a Mean—\operatorname{Var}(\bar X)=\sigma^2/n—is a theorem about sums, and the central limit theorem that followed is a theorem about sums too. Sums are what the mathematics could reach.

The questions people ask are not confined to that list, and this chapter is about what happens when your question walks off the edge of it.

A number the mean cannot describe

A small charity has 30 donations from its first campaign, in dollars.

Most gifts are between $10 and $45. A few are much larger, and one is $348.70. This is the shape almost every money variable has, and it is why Describing a Single Variable spent time on skew.

The mean is $57.19 and the median is $26.65. They differ by more than a factor of two, and only one of them describes a donor:

Eight of the thirty donors—27%—gave more than the average donation. If the director asks what does a typical supporter give?, the answer is $26.65 and it is not close. Reporting $57.19 would have the fundraising team writing appeals aimed at a donor who barely exists.

So you report the median. And then the director, who has been well trained, asks the Describing a Single Variable question:

If we ran the campaign again with 30 different donors, how different would that $26.65 be?

You have nothing. There is no median row in the standard error table. What you can do is the thing you were taught, which is the wrong thing:

An interval from $28.09 to $86.30—for the mean, which is not the number you decided to report, and which is nearly useless anyway: drop the single $348.70 gift and s falls from 77.95 to 56.16, so a quarter of the width of that interval is one donor.

Two separate failures, and this chapter fixes both
  1. The statistic you want has no formula. Median, correlation, 90th percentile, ratio of two rates, difference of two medians, interquartile range—no standard error in your toolkit, and for several of them no simple one exists at all.
  2. Where a formula does exist, its assumptions can fail. The t-interval above is entitled to its 95% only under conditions this sample plainly does not meet. The next section measures exactly how badly.

“But surely there is a formula for the median”

There is, and looking at it is instructive. For a large sample from a population with density f, the standard error of the sample median is approximately

\operatorname{SE}(\text{median}) \approx \frac{1}{2f(m)\sqrt{n}}

where m is the population median and f(m) is the height of the population density at that point. Note what the formula needs: not the population variance, which you can estimate easily, but the population density at a single point, which you cannot. Estimating a density well is a harder problem than the one you started with, and it requires choices—bandwidths, kernels—that change the answer.

So the formula exists on paper and dissolves in your hands. That is the normal situation off the edge of the list, and it is worth knowing that the reason we teach means is not that means are the most useful summary. It is that means are the ones somebody could do the algebra for.

Where the formula exists and is still wrong

Take the second failure on its own, because it is the one people do not expect.

Forget medians. Suppose you want the mean, the formula does apply, and you build a t-interval exactly as taught. How often does it actually contain the truth?

We can check, because we can be the population. Draw a sample of 8 from a skewed population whose true mean we know, build the 95% t-interval, and ask whether the truth is inside. Then do that 4,000 times and count.

72.9%. The procedure advertises 95% and delivers 73%. More than a quarter of the intervals miss, and a reader who took the interval at face value would be wrong about four times as often as they were told to expect.

This is not a bug in R and it is not a bad seed. Run the identical code on a Normal population:

94.75%, which is 95% to within simulation noise. The formula is fine. The formula’s assumptions were fine in the second case and not in the first, and nothing in the output of t.test() will ever tell you which case you are in.

Warning

This is what “the assumptions failed” means in practice. Not an error message. Not a warning. A number, printed confidently, whose stated properties are false.

What we actually need

Step back from formulas and ask what the question is.

You have a statistic—call it \hat\theta, whether that is a mean, a median, a correlation, or anything else. It was computed from one sample. A different sample would have given a different value. The distribution of those values, across all the samples you might have drawn, is the sampling distribution, and every give-or-take in statistics is a summary of it: its spread is a standard error, its middle 95% is a confidence interval.

The Standard Error of a Mean obtained that distribution directly, by brute force:

```{r}
sample_means <- replicate(5000, mean(rnorm(n, mean = 100, sd = sigma)))
sd(sample_means)
```

Draw a fresh sample from the population, compute the statistic, repeat 5,000 times, look at the spread. No algebra, no assumptions, and it works for any statistic—swap mean for median and the code still runs:

There it is. The sampling distribution of the median of 30 donations sits at $37.82, against a true population median of $36.60, with a spread of $9.41—so a standard error for a median, obtained with no theory whatsoever.

And it is useless, because of one line: rlnorm(30, meanlog = 3.6, sdlog = 1.1). That line draws a fresh sample from the population. In this chapter we could write it because we invented the population. In your project you cannot. You have 30 donations and there is no going back to the well.

The substitution

Here is the whole of confidence intervals.

The plug-in idea

We need to draw samples from the population, and we cannot.

But we have 30 observations that came from that population, and they are the only information about it in existence. So we use them as a stand-in: we treat the sample as though it were the population, and draw from that.

Drawing n observations from a pile of n observations, in a way that lets the same one come up more than once, is exactly sampling with replacement.

Compare the two lines directly:

The population version (The Standard Error of a Mean, impossible in practice) The bootstrap version (available now)
Draw a sample rlnorm(30, 3.6, 1.1) sample(donation, 30, replace = TRUE)
Compute the statistic median(...) median(...)
Repeat replicate(4000, ...) replicate(4000, ...)
Read off the spread sd(...) sd(...)

One cell changed. Everything else is identical—and the one that changed is the one you could not run.

So the charity’s answer is: the typical gift is about $27, and a repeat campaign of the same size would plausibly put that figure anywhere between $19 and $41. That is an honest sentence about a median, produced with no formula, no distributional assumption, and four lines of base R.

The details—why this works, how far to trust it, and where it fails—are 6.2. What matters here is that you can see where the idea came from: it is the The Standard Error of a Mean simulation with the one impossible step replaced by the best available substitute.

The name is Bradley Efron’s, from 1979, and it is a joke about pulling yourself up by your own bootstraps—getting something from apparently nothing. The something is not free, and the next section is the bill.

What the substitution buys, and what it does not

The substitution is sample for population. Everything the bootstrap tells you is conditional on that swap being reasonable.

What you get. If your sample was drawn fairly from the population you care about, the empirical distribution of your data is a genuinely good stand-in for the population distribution, and it gets better as n grows. The variability you observe across resamples then really does approximate the variability you would see across fresh samples. That is a theorem, and it is why the bootstrap is not a trick.

What you do not get. Anything about whether you sampled the right people.

Watch this fail. A campus study advertises for volunteers, and—as volunteers do—only people at the higher end of the scale bother to respond.

The population mean is 100.04. The survey mean is 117.07—wrong by 17 points, and no analysis of these 60 numbers can know that.

Now bootstrap it, carefully and correctly:

A 95% interval of (115.11, 119.16), four points wide, beautifully tight, and it does not contain the true mean of 100. It never will. Every resample is drawn from the same 60 volunteers, so every resample inherits their tilt, and running 100,000 resamples instead of 10,000 would narrow the interval further while leaving it centered on the wrong number.

The sentence to memorize

The bootstrap quantifies sampling variability. It does not detect, correct, or warn you about bias.

A tight bootstrap interval says your number is reproducible. It says nothing about whether your number is right.

This should feel familiar. It is the same warning attached to \sigma/\sqrt{n} in The Standard Error of a Mean—two million self-selected respondents give a gorgeous standard error and can be wrong by twenty points—and it is the same warning as the Aug 31 session on where the data came from. Selection bias, survivorship bias, and non-response are properties of how the rows got into your file. Resampling those rows cannot undo them, because the resampling never leaves the file.

So the Aug 31 questions do not go away this week; they get more important, not less. Who is missing from this sample? is the question no amount of computation answers, and a narrow interval around a biased estimate is worse than a wide one, because it is more persuasive.

A narrow interval around the wrong number

A campus wellness study advertises on Instagram and collects 400 responses. The sample mean sleep duration is 7.6 hours. A bootstrap with 10,000 resamples gives a 95% percentile interval of (7.5, 7.7).

A colleague concludes: “The interval is narrow, so we can be confident the average student sleeps about 7.6 hours.”

What is wrong with that reasoning?

The bootstrap treats your sample as a stand-in for the population and then draws from it. Everything it tells you is downstream of that substitution.

If the substitution is good—if your 400 responses look like the population in the ways that matter—then the spread of the bootstrap distribution is a genuine estimate of how much your statistic would move if you collected the data again, and the percentile interval means what you want it to mean.

If the substitution is bad, the bootstrap does not notice. It cannot: the 400 responses are all the information it is given. A self-selected sample that runs half an hour high will produce resamples that all run half an hour high, and the interval will be tight around 7.6 when the truth is 7.1.

Important

The bootstrap quantifies sampling variability. It does not detect, correct, or warn you about bias.

This is the same lesson as \sigma/\sqrt{n} from Week 5, in new clothes. A survey of two million self-selected respondents has a beautiful standard error and can be wrong by twenty points. Narrowness is a statement about reproducibility, not about truth—and a narrow interval around a biased estimate is worse than a wide one, because it is more persuasive.

The remedy is never more resampling. It is the Aug 31 question: where did this data come from, and who is missing from it?

Exercise: measure the coverage yourself

The chapter claimed a 95% t-interval covers only about 73% of the time on small skewed samples. Do not take it on faith—the function below runs one such study and reports whether its interval caught the truth.

```{r}
mean(replicate(4000, one_study()))
```

About 0.73. The gap between the claimed 0.95 and the delivered 0.73 is the entire motivation for this week.

Exercise: make the substitution

This is the move the whole week rests on. In 6.1 we wanted the sampling distribution of the median and could not draw fresh samples from the population. Do it with the sample instead.

```{r}
sd(replicate(4000, median(sample(donation, 30, replace = TRUE))))
```

About 5.3. Writing length(donation) in place of the hard-coded 30 is the better habit—the resample must be the same size as the original sample, and hard-coding that number is how it silently stops being true.

Exercise: what is this interval entitled to say?

answer <- "B"

The interval quantifies variability under repetition of this recruitment procedure. Repeating a tilted procedure gives a tilted answer every time, and tightly.

Summary

  • The standard error machinery of The Standard Error of a Mean covers means and things that are means in disguise—proportions, differences of means, regression coefficients. Medians, correlations, percentiles, and ratios are outside it.
  • Formulas for those statistics either do not exist or need quantities you cannot estimate—the median’s standard error needs the population density at the median.
  • Where a formula does exist, its assumptions can fail silently. A 95% t-interval at n = 8 on a skewed population covered the truth 72.9% of the time; the same code on a Normal population covered 94.75%. Nothing in the output distinguishes the two cases.
  • What every give-or-take needs is the sampling distribution of the statistic. The Standard Error of a Mean built it by drawing many samples from a known population. You have one sample and no population.
  • The substitution: treat the sample as a stand-in for the population and draw from it—which is sampling with replacement, n at a time. One line of the The Standard Error of a Mean simulation changes; the rest is identical.
  • It works for any statistic, because nothing in the loop depends on which statistic you chose.
  • It quantifies sampling variability and nothing else. A bootstrap of a self-selected sample gave a 95% interval of (115.11, 119.16) around a true mean of 100. Narrow, reproducible, and wrong. Aug 31’s question—who is missing from this data?—is not answerable by resampling.
