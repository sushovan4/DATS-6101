Practice Problems


28 minutes

None of these are graded and none of them are auto-checked. They exist so that you arrive on Monday with something to argue about.

The first section comes in two halves as usual: predict, then run. Write the prediction down before you press Run—resampling output is particularly easy to nod along with after the fact, because every number is plausible and none of them is checkable by eye.

This runs and is still wrong is the important half this week. Every cell in it executes cleanly, produces a well-formatted number, and is wrong. Resampling code fails silently more readily than almost anything else you will write: there is no formula to sanity-check against, no warning, and the output is always a nice small number that looks like a standard error.

What to bring to class

For each problem, bring an answer you are prepared to be wrong about.

And bring the last item, C5, in written form. Monday’s lab is bootstrapping your own project’s statistic, and C5 is the preparation for it.

Predict before you run

A1. Which statistic is estimated most precisely?

Sixty salaries, heavily right-skewed. The same bootstrap is run three times, on the mean, the median, and the 10% trimmed mean.

Predict the ordering of the three standard errors before running, and say why.

Then answer: the median came out with a standard error about 25% smaller than the mean’s. Explain that in terms of what a resample does to a long right tail—which observations move the mean around, and how often do they appear in a resample? Then say why “the median is more precise” is nonetheless not a general reason to prefer medians, by naming a population where the ordering would reverse.

A2. The resample size is not a free parameter

x has 100 observations. Three bootstraps, differing only in how many observations each resample contains.

Predict all three numbers, at least as ratios, from \sigma/\sqrt{n}.

Then answer: only one of the first three is the standard error of your estimate. Say which, and say precisely what question the other two answer. Then explain why size_400 is not a way of getting a more precise result out of 100 observations, in a sentence you could say to a manager who suggests it.

A3. B and n do different jobs

Four bootstraps: two sample sizes crossed with two values of B.

Predict which of the four numbers are about equal to each other, and in what pattern, before running.

Then answer: the four numbers form two pairs, not four distinct values. Say what each of n and B controls, and what a hundredfold increase in B bought in each row. Then: if the numbers within a pair are not exactly equal, what is the size of that discrepancy a measurement of?

A4. Two ways to shuffle

A permutation test can shuffle the labels or shuffle the outcomes.

Predict whether the two p-values will differ.

Then answer: the two agree to within simulation noise, and there is a reason that is not a coincidence. State it. Then say what quantity the small remaining gap between 0.0466 and 0.0414 is measuring, and how you would make it go away.

A5. The bootstrap meets the minimum

Sixty measurements from a population whose true minimum is 5.

Predict the lower endpoint of the interval before running.

Then answer: the lower endpoint is 5.4 and the median of the bootstrap distribution is also 5.4. Explain both, and then state the general condition from The Bootstrap that this violates. Finally: the upper endpoint, 8.4, is a perfectly sensible number. Why does the interval fail on one side and not the other?

This runs, and it is still wrong

Every cell below executes without an error or a warning. Find the problem, say what the number actually measures, and fix it.

B1. The correlation with a confidence interval around zero

Task: 50 stores’ advertising spend and sales. Report the correlation and a 95% bootstrap interval for it.

The observed correlation is 0.8645 and the “95% interval” is (-0.275, 0.275)—an interval that does not contain the estimate, and that comfortably contains zero.

Answer: what is boot_r actually the sampling distribution of? Point at the exact line that causes it, say why the center came out at 0.0008 rather than 0.86, and write the corrected cell. Then say what feature of the output should have stopped the analyst before they read the interval at all.

B2. The suspiciously precise response time

Task: 80 API response times. Report the standard error of the mean.

3.621 milliseconds. And the correct answer:

Answer: name the single wrong number in the first cell. Then: the wrong answer is smaller than the right one by a factor of about 3.5. Predict that factor from \sigma/\sqrt{n} before checking it against 12.545/3.621, and say which study the 3.621 is the standard error of. Finally, the general rule from 6.2 that this breaks, in one sentence.

B3. The panel that answered

Task: estimate mean weekly grocery spending for a city. A loyalty-card panel yields 900 respondents; report the mean with a bootstrap interval.

Answer: the bootstrap code is entirely correct and the reported interval misses the truth. Say what the interval is a valid statement about, and name the population it actually describes. Then: the analyst proposes raising the panel to 9,000 respondents. What happens to the interval, what happens to the error, and what would you spend the money on instead? Tie your answer to the Aug 31 material by name.

B4. Before and after

Task: 30 patients had blood pressure measured before and after eight weeks on a drug. Test whether the drug changed it.

An observed drop of 3.73 mmHg and p = 0.4644. Now the correct test, which shuffles only what the null says is interchangeable—the order within each patient:

Zero of 5,000.

Answer: the first test says nothing is happening and the second says the effect is overwhelming. Explain the gap in terms of what the free shuffle put into the null distribution that the paired shuffle kept out—compute sd(pre) and sd(d) if it helps. Which assumption from Permutation Tests did the first cell break? And, on reporting: the second test found 0 of 5,000, so what number goes in the paper?

Task: 400 daily readings from a sensor. Report the standard error of the mean daily level.

Judgment

No code. Bring written answers; we will disagree about these out loud.

C1. The wrong tool

A civil engineer has 150 annual peak river levels and needs the level that will be exceeded once in 200 years, for a flood defense specification. She bootstraps the 99.5th percentile of her 150 observations and reports a 95% interval.

Say what is structurally wrong with this, in terms of where the information for that number would have to come from. Then: is her problem the same as the maximum problem from The Bootstrap, or a different one? Finally, and most importantly, write the two sentences she should put in her report instead—remembering that “the data cannot answer this” is a finding, not a failure, and that a defense will be built either way.

C2. Which interval goes in the report?

You have n = 22 observations, mildly right-skewed. The t-interval is (41.2, 58.8). The bootstrap percentile interval is (42.6, 59.9).

Neither is obviously right. Argue for one, then argue for the other, then say what you would actually write—and what you would put in the methods section so that a reader could tell what you did. Then answer the question a colleague will ask: “Why not just report whichever is narrower?”

C3. The A/B test that nearly worked

6.3’s landing-page test gave a 24% relative lift, 38 clicks against 47, and p = 0.365. The product manager says: “The permutation test is more powerful than the old proportion test, so let’s rerun the analysis that way and see.”

Two things are wrong with that sentence. Name both. Then use \sigma/\sqrt{n} to estimate roughly how many visitors per arm would have been needed to detect a 1.5-point lift on a 6% base rate, and say what you would have told the team before launch. Finally, connect this to the peeking problem from A/B testing: what is the specific temptation created by a p-value of 0.365, and why does resampling do nothing about it?

C4. “Resampling makes no assumptions”

You will read this sentence in blog posts and hear it in interviews.

List what the bootstrap actually assumes, and what a permutation test actually assumes. For each, give a concrete situation from this week’s chapters where the assumption fails and the method returns a confident wrong answer. Then rewrite the sentence so that it is both short and true.

C5. Your own project statistic

This is the preparation for Monday’s lab, so do it in writing.

Pick one number from your project data that is not a mean—a median, a correlation, a percentile, a ratio of two rates, a difference between two groups, whatever your question actually needs.

  1. Write the statistic as a one-line R expression on your data.
  2. Say whether you need an interval or a p-value, and therefore whether you will bootstrap or permute. Justify the choice in one sentence.
  3. Write down the check you will run first: bootstrap a mean from the same data and compare it with s/\sqrt{n}.
  4. Name the one thing about how your data was collected that your interval will not account for. Every dataset has one.

Bring all four. Item 4 is the one we will spend the most time on.
