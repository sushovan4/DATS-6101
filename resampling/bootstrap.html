The Bootstrap


47 minutes

Why Resample argued for the substitution. This chapter does it properly: the algorithm, a check against a case where we already know the answer, two statistics no formula would have given us, and the places where the whole thing falls over.

The algorithm

The bootstrap, in four steps

You have a sample x_1, \ldots, x_n and a statistic \hat\theta computed from it.

  1. Draw n observations from your sample with replacement. Call it a resample.
  2. Compute the statistic on the resample.
  3. Repeat steps 1–2 B times, collecting B values \hat\theta^*_1, \ldots, \hat\theta^*_B.
  4. The spread of those B values estimates the spread of the sampling distribution of \hat\theta.

In R, that is one line:

```{r}
boot <- replicate(B, statistic(sample(x, length(x), replace = TRUE)))
```

Three details of step 1 matter and are worth stating before anything else runs.

The resample is the same size as the original sample, n. Not 1,000, not n/2. The bootstrap is imitating the act of collecting a sample of size n, and the width of a sampling distribution depends on n. Change the resample size and you are estimating the standard error of a study you did not run.

With replacement. Without it, drawing n from n just reorders the data and nothing varies at all. There is a section on this below, because it is the single most common bug in bootstrap code and it fails silently.

Whatever statistic is. Nothing in the loop knows or cares. That is the entire point of the week.

One resample, looked at closely

Forty-eight patients’ waiting times at a walk-in clinic, in minutes:

Mean 11.45 minutes, and because this is a mean we already have its standard error from The Standard Error of a Mean: s/\sqrt{n} = 0.4714. Hold on to that number; it is the answer key.

Now draw one resample and look at it:

Read that resample. 9.7 appears four times, 11.5 five times, and a substantial fraction of the original values do not appear at all—only 25 of the 48 distinct values made it in. The resample is not a copy of the data. It is the same data with the weights jostled, and its mean came out 11.38 rather than 11.45.

That jostling is the mechanism. Each observation is included a random number of times, so each resample is a slightly different “study”, and the variation across resamples stands in for the variation across studies.

How much of the data shows up in a typical resample?

About 63.6%, and not by accident. Any particular observation is missed on a single draw with probability 1 - 1/n, so it is missed by all n draws with probability (1-1/n)^n, which tends to e^{-1} \approx 0.368. So the expected fraction included is 1 - (1-1/n)^n \to 1 - 1/e = 0.632, essentially regardless of n. Roughly a third of your data sits out every resample, and that is by design.

Does it actually work?

Here is the move that should earn the bootstrap your trust, and it is worth doing before you use the method on anything you care about.

We are about to bootstrap a mean—a statistic whose standard error we already know from The Standard Error of a Mean. If the bootstrap is sound, it must reproduce s/\sqrt{n}. If it does not, nothing else it tells us is worth reading.

0.4634 against 0.4714, a ratio of 0.983. Two completely different routes to the same number: one is four lines of algebra about variances of sums, the other is a computer shuffling a vector ten thousand times and never touching a formula.

The 1.7% gap is not simulation noise—it is systematic and it is explicable. The bootstrap resamples from the empirical distribution, whose variance is \frac{n-1}{n}s^2 rather than s^2, so the bootstrap SE targets \sqrt{(n-1)/n}\cdot s/\sqrt n = 0.4665, about 1% below the formula. At n = 48 that is invisible in practice; at n = 6 it is not, which is one reason small samples appear in the failure list below.

Here is the bootstrap distribution:

Compare it, mentally, with the histograms in The Standard Error of a Mean. It is the same picture: a roughly Normal pile of statistic-values, centered on the estimate, whose width is the standard error. The difference is that 5.3 built it by going back to the population 5,000 times and this one never left the 48 observations.

The percentile interval

Once you have B bootstrapped values, an interval requires no further theory: take the middle 95% of them.

That is the percentile interval: (10.535, 12.354). Against the t-interval for the same data:

(10.502, 12.398). The two agree to within about four hundredths of a minute—two and a half seconds—on an interval nearly two minutes wide.

They differ slightly, and in a predictable direction: the bootstrap interval is a touch narrower, because the t-interval multiplies by t_{47,0.975} = 2.011 to account for having estimated \sigma from the data, while the percentile interval reads its endpoints off the resamples and inherits the same \sqrt{(n-1)/n} shrinkage noted above. At n = 48 that is a rounding error. At n = 10 it is not, and the t-interval is the safer choice whenever its assumptions hold.

Where both apply, use the formula. The bootstrap is not a replacement for t-intervals on well-behaved means; it is what you reach for when there is no formula, or when you have reason to believe the formula’s assumptions are false. Agreement on the easy cases is what licenses you to trust it on the hard ones.

A median, where nothing else was available

Back to the 30 donations from Why Resample, and the question the director actually asked.

A 95% interval of ($18.85, $40.85) for the median gift. Notice something the formula approach could never have produced:

The interval reaches $7.80 below the estimate and $14.20 above it. It is asymmetric, and it should be—the population is right-skewed, so the median of a resample can wander further up than down. Any interval of the form “estimate \pm 2\,\text{SE}” is symmetric by construction and would have got this wrong in both directions at once.

Look at the distribution and you can see why an interval is the right output here and a standard error alone is not:

It is lumpy. The median of 30 numbers is always the average of the 15th and 16th order statistics, so it can only take a limited set of values—the 10,000 resamples produced 99 distinct medians and no others. That granularity is real, not an artifact, and it is one more thing a smooth formula would have concealed.

A correlation, and the trap in it

45 students’ study hours and exam scores:

r = 0.7666. How precise is that?

There is a formula—Fisher’s z transformation, which cor.test() uses—and it requires the pair (x, y) to be bivariate Normal, an assumption almost nobody checks and which fails routinely for bounded scores and skewed predictors. Bootstrap it instead.

The trap: a correlation is a property of pairs. If you resample hours and score independently you destroy the pairing and measure the correlation of unrelated columns, which is zero by construction. So resample the row indices and carry both columns along:

A 95% interval of (0.651, 0.856). And the formula’s answer, for comparison:

(0.610, 0.865). The same story—both say “strong, and pinned down to about \pm 0.1”—with the bootstrap interval slightly narrower on the low side. Two methods, one assumption-laden and one not, landing in the same place is the outcome you want to see, and when they disagree markedly you have learned something worth chasing.

Resample rows, not columns

Whenever your statistic involves more than one variable—a correlation, a regression slope, a difference of group means, a ratio of two totals—resample observations, not each column separately. The unit you resample must be the unit that was independently sampled in the first place.

This is the single most common conceptual error in student bootstrap code, and it does not throw an error. It quietly returns the sampling distribution of a statistic computed on data with the relationship torn out of it.

How many resamples?

B is not n, and confusing the two is the second most common error.

n is fixed by your data and determines how wide the bootstrap distribution is—that is the thing you are trying to measure. B is chosen by you and determines how precisely you have measured it. Raising B costs seconds and buys you a stabler answer to the same question; it does not buy you a better study.

Watch. Five independent runs of the same bootstrap at B = 200 and at B = 20{,}000, each reporting the upper endpoint of the 95% interval:

run 1 run 2 run 3 run 4 run 5 spread
B = 200 12.376 12.315 12.353 12.380 12.250 0.130
B = 20{,}000 12.354 12.352 12.350 12.363 12.348 0.015

Both rows sit at about 12.35, which is the honest answer given these 48 observations. The top row wobbles by 0.13 depending on nothing but the random seed; the bottom row wobbles by 0.015. That is the 1/\sqrt{B} from The Standard Error of a Mean again—a hundredfold increase in B divides the Monte Carlo error by ten.

Rules of thumb
  • B = 1{,}000 is plenty for a standard error, which is a summary of the bulk of the distribution.
  • B = 10{,}000 for a 95% interval, whose endpoints depend on the sparse tails and are noisier.
  • Report B alongside the interval, and use set.seed() so the number in your report is the number a reader reproduces.
  • If someone asks you to narrow an interval, more resamples is never the answer. More data is.
Turning up B

You have n = 40 observations and you run a bootstrap with B = 1{,}000 resamples, getting a 95% percentile interval of width 3.2. Your colleague suggests raising B to 100,000.

What will that change?

There are two sources of randomness here, and keeping them apart is the point of the question.

The sample size n sets how much the statistic really would move if the study were repeated. That is the quantity you are trying to estimate, and it is fixed the moment your data is collected. No amount of resampling improves it.

The number of resamples B sets how well you have measured that quantity. Each resample is one draw from the bootstrap distribution, and, as in Week 5, the Monte Carlo error in any summary of those draws falls like 1/\sqrt{B}.

So raising B from 1,000 to 100,000 divides the run-to-run wobble in the endpoints by ten, and leaves the width essentially where it was. If you want a narrower interval you need more data, not more resamples.

A practical rule: B = 1{,}000 is enough for a standard error, and B = 10{,}000 is comfortable for a 95% interval, whose endpoints depend on the sparse tails of the distribution and so are noisier than its middle. Resamples are cheap. Observations are not.

Why the replacement is the whole mechanism

Predict this before you run it. The only change is replace = FALSE.

Exactly zero, and one distinct value. Drawing 48 items from a pile of 48 without replacement returns all 48 items in a new order. The mean does not look at order. So all 1,000 “resamples” are the same number, their standard deviation is 0, and the code cheerfully reports that your estimate has no uncertainty whatsoever.

No error. No warning. A perfectly formatted zero.

The check that catches it

A bootstrap standard error of exactly 0 is always a bug. So is one that is absurdly small compared with s/\sqrt{n}.

Better still, run the test from earlier in this chapter on your own data: bootstrap the mean and compare it with s/\sqrt{n}. If those two disagree badly, your bootstrap code is wrong, and you have found out before you trusted it with the statistic you actually care about.

The missing argument

A student writes a bootstrap and forgets one argument:

boot <- replicate(2000, mean(sample(x, length(x), replace = FALSE)))
sd(boot)

x has 48 observations. What does sd(boot) print, and why?

Drawing 48 items from a set of 48 without replacement is a shuffle. You get every observation back exactly once, in a new order, and every summary that ignores order—the mean, the median, the standard deviation, the correlation with a correspondingly shuffled partner—is bit-for-bit identical every time.

So the 2,000 replicates are 2,000 copies of one number, and their standard deviation is exactly 0. The code runs, prints something, and reports that your estimate has no uncertainty at all. That is the most dangerous kind of bug.

Replacement is not a technicality; it is the entire mechanism. With replacement, some observations appear twice or three times and others not at all—on average about 63% of the original values appear in any given resample. That reshuffling of weights is what makes one resample differ from the next, and the variation across resamples is the whole estimate.

The way to catch this in your own code is to check that your bootstrap standard error is not zero, and—better—to check the one case where you know the answer: the bootstrap SE of a mean should land close to s/\sqrt{n}.

Where the bootstrap breaks

It is a general method, not a universal one. Two failures are worth knowing by name, because both produce output that looks fine.

Very small n

The bootstrap can only ever redistribute the values you have. With five observations, there are only 126 distinct multisets of size 5 to draw, so the “sampling distribution” is a handful of points pretending to be a curve.

10,000 resamples, 116 distinct answers. And the interval:

The percentile interval is (41.96, 54.62); the t-interval is (36.77, 57.35), more than 60% wider. The bootstrap interval is too narrow, and the reason is structural rather than random: the resamples can never contain a value more extreme than the largest observation, so at tiny n the tails of the true sampling distribution are simply unrepresented. There is nothing in the data to build them from.

Below roughly n = 20, be careful; below n = 10, the bootstrap is not rescuing you and you should say what your sample size is and let the reader discount accordingly.

Statistics that depend on the extremes

This one is more interesting, because it fails no matter how large n is.

Suppose you want the maximum of a population—the peak load a system has to survive, the largest claim an insurer might face. Forty observations from a population whose true maximum is 100:

The largest value we saw is 98. Bootstrap it:

Look at what happened. The bootstrap distribution takes nine distinct values, 64% of them exactly 98, and the 95% interval is (90.8, 98.0). Its upper endpoint is the observed maximum—and it always will be, because no resample can contain a value larger than the largest observation. The true maximum of 100 lies outside the interval, and no increase in B or n will ever let the interval reach it.

That 64% is not a coincidence either: it is the 1 - (1-1/n)^n \approx 0.632 from earlier. The largest observation appears in about 63% of resamples, and whenever it does, the resample maximum is exactly it.

The general condition

The bootstrap works for statistics that are smooth functions of the data—means, medians, correlations, quantiles away from the edges, regression coefficients, ratios. Perturb the data slightly and the statistic moves slightly.

It fails for statistics that depend on the extreme order statistics: the maximum, the minimum, the range, the 99.9th percentile of 200 observations. For those, the empirical distribution is a bad stand-in exactly where it matters, because your sample has no information at all about the region beyond its own edge. Extreme-value theory exists for this reason.

It also fails when the observations are not independent—time series with autocorrelation, students clustered in classrooms, repeated measures on the same subject. Resampling individual rows destroys the dependence, and the resulting standard error is too small, in exactly the way Step 2 of the The Standard Error of a Mean derivation was too small. (There are block bootstraps for this; they resample contiguous chunks instead of rows.)

The recipe

For anything you will meet this term:

```{r}
set.seed(6101)
B <- 10000
n <- length(x)

boot <- replicate(B, {
  i <- sample(n, n, replace = TRUE)   # resample ROW INDICES
  statistic(x[i])                     # ... or f(x[i], y[i]) for paired data
})

sd(boot)                              # standard error
quantile(boot, c(0.025, 0.975))       # 95% percentile interval
```

Then, before you believe a word of it: run the same code with statistic set to mean and check it against sd(x)/sqrt(n).

Exercises

Exercise: bootstrap a standard error

```{r}
sd(replicate(2000, mean(sample(wait, length(wait), replace = TRUE))))
```

About 0.46, against s/\sqrt{n} = 0.4714.

Exercise: break it on purpose

Predict the answer before you run this one. It is the same code as the last exercise with one argument changed.

```{r}
sd(replicate(2000, mean(sample(wait, 48, replace = FALSE))))
```

Exactly 0. Each “resample” is a permutation of the original 48 values, so every resample mean equals mean(wait).

Exercise: an interval for a statistic with no formula

```{r}
boot <- replicate(4000, quantile(sample(wait, length(wait), replace = TRUE), 0.9))
quantile(boot, c(0.025, 0.975))
```

About (13.7, 17.2). The 90th percentile is far enough from the edge of the data that the bootstrap is comfortable here—unlike the maximum, which is at the edge.

Exercise: bootstrap a correlation

```{r}
sd(replicate(2000, {
  i <- sample(length(hours), length(hours), replace = TRUE)
  cor(hours[i], score[i])
}))
```

About 0.053. The single index vector i is what keeps each student’s hours attached to that same student’s score.

The maximum is a function of the extreme order statistic, and the empirical distribution has nothing beyond its own largest value. Smooth statistics are fine; edge statistics are not.

Summary

  • The algorithm: resample n observations with replacement, compute the statistic, repeat B times, summarize the spread. One line of R, and indifferent to which statistic you chose.
  • About 63.2% of the original observations appear in a typical resample. That jostling of weights is the mechanism.
  • The trust check: bootstrapping a mean gave SE 0.4634 against the The Standard Error of a Mean formula’s 0.4714—agreement to within 2%, by two routes that share nothing. Run this check on your own data before believing anything else.
  • Percentile interval: the middle 95% of the bootstrap distribution. On the waiting times it matched the t-interval to within 2.5 seconds. On the donations it produced a properly asymmetric interval, ($18.85, $40.85) around a median of $26.65, which no \pm 2\,\text{SE} rule could give.
  • Resample rows, not columns. Resampling hours and score separately measures the wobble of a correlation of zero.
  • B is not n. n sets the width of the interval; B sets how precisely you have measured it, with Monte Carlo error falling like 1/\sqrt{B}. Use B = 10{,}000 for an interval and stop worrying about it.
  • Without replacement, everything collapses to a single number and the SE is exactly 0. Silently.
  • It breaks at very small n (five observations give 116 distinct resample means and an interval 60% too narrow), for statistics driven by the extremes (the bootstrap interval for a maximum has the observed maximum as its upper endpoint, always), and for dependent observations.
