Permutation Tests


36 minutes

In the hypothesis-testing week you learned that a p-value is the probability of observing data at least as extreme as yours, if the null hypothesis were true. That sentence is correct, and for most people it stays a sentence. The null hypothesis is somewhere off in the distance being true, a formula converts your data into a t, and a function returns a number.

This chapter removes the formula. The null hypothesis becomes something you can do—a physical operation on your own data, which you can watch, run ten thousand times, and count. Nothing here requires a distribution, a degrees of freedom, or an assumption about shape.

If p-values felt mystical in October, this is where they stop being.

The experiment

A product team randomized 33 new users to the old onboarding flow or a redesigned one, and measured minutes of engagement in the first week.

The new flow is ahead by 10.9 minutes. Eighteen users and fifteen users, and group standard deviations of 12.3 and 9.4—so the groups overlap heavily and 10.9 is not obviously beyond what shuffling people around could produce.

That is exactly the question: could a gap this large have arisen with no real difference between the flows at all?

The null hypothesis, as an instruction

Say the null hypothesis out loud, carefully:

The redesign has no effect. Each user would have engaged for the same number of minutes whichever flow they had been assigned to.

Now notice what that implies about your data file. If a user’s outcome does not depend on their assignment, then the labels "old" and "new" are just tags that a randomizer stuck on afterwards. Any other assignment of those same 18 and 15 tags to those same 33 outcomes was equally likely to have happened.

That is not a metaphor. The randomization genuinely could have come out differently, and under the null the numbers would have been identical—only the tags would have moved. So we can generate the alternative worlds ourselves:

In that particular alternative world, the “new” group came out 6.09 minutes worse. Nothing about the users changed. Only the labels moved.

The core move

sample(arm) shuffles the labels without replacement, so exactly 18 users are called "old" and 15 are called "new", as in the real study. The outcomes never move.

Do that ten thousand times and you have built, from your own data and with no theory at all, the distribution of results the null hypothesis predicts.

Ten thousand alternative worlds

Two things to read off before going further.

It is centered at zero—to three decimal places. That is not luck; it is the null hypothesis made visible. Shuffling cannot create a systematic advantage for either label, so the average shuffled difference is zero by construction. This is the sharpest contrast with the bootstrap, whose distribution is centered at your observed statistic.

Its spread is 4.31 minutes. That is a standard error, obtained without a formula: it is how much a difference of means bounces around at these sample sizes and this variability, when nothing real is going on.

Here is the picture, with the observed difference marked:

Chance alone routinely produces gaps of 4 or 5 minutes and occasionally 12. Our 10.9 sits well out in the right tail. The whole question is how far out.

The p-value, as a counting exercise

100 out of 10,000, so p = 0.010.

Read that sentence again, because it is the definition of a p-value with the mathematics taken out: in one hundred of ten thousand worlds where the labels meant nothing, the gap was at least as big as the one we saw.

That is all a p-value has ever been. No t distribution, no degrees of freedom, no assumption about the shape of anything.

Two-sided, and why abs()

We counted shuffles where |{\text{difference}}| \geq |10.9|—that is, worlds where the gap was at least as extreme in either direction. That is the two-sided p-value, and it is what you want unless you genuinely committed, in advance, to caring only about one direction. Dropping the abs() roughly halves the p-value, and doing so after seeing which way the result went is one of the ways researchers fool themselves.

A permutation p-value is never exactly 0

If none of your 10,000 shuffles is as extreme as the observed value, the honest report is p < 0.0001, not p = 0. Some analysts use (1 + \#\{\text{as extreme}\})/(B+1) for this reason, which cannot return zero. The finite B has put a floor on the resolution, and that floor is your reporting limit.

The same data, through a t-test

Now the comparison that matters, because it tells you what you have gained and what you have not.

The Welch t-test gives p = 0.0072. The permutation test gave p = 0.010. The same conclusion, from two procedures with almost nothing in common.

Permutation test Welch t-test
p-value 0.010 0.0072
Null distribution built by shuffling your data assumed to be t with 30.8 df
Assumes near-Normality no yes, or a large n
Assumes equal variances no no (Welch)
Assumes exchangeability under the null yes implicitly
Works for the median instead yes, unchanged no

The agreement is the point, in the same way as 6.2’s mean check. Two methods that share no machinery landing on the same answer is what tells you the permutation machinery is sound—and once it is sound, you can point it at questions the t-test cannot reach.

Where the t-test’s assumptions hold, it is a fine tool and a great deal faster. The permutation test earns its keep when n is small, when the data are plainly not Normal, or when the statistic you care about is not a difference of means at all.

What it assumes

“Assumption-free” is a slogan you will see attached to permutation tests, and it is not true. There is exactly one assumption, and it is a real one.

Exchangeability under the null

If the null hypothesis is true, then every relabelling you generate must have been equally likely to occur.

In a randomized experiment this is not an assumption at all—it is a description of what the experimenter did. That is why permutation tests and randomized experiments fit together so naturally: the test reruns the randomization.

Where it fails, it fails hard:

  • Paired or repeated measures. Thirty patients measured before and after treatment: shuffling all 60 labels freely asserts that patient 7’s “before” could have been patient 22’s “after”. It could not. The correct test flips a coin within each pair.
  • Clustered data. Students within classrooms, patients within hospitals. The unit that was randomized is the cluster, so the cluster is what you shuffle.
  • Non-random assignment. If the “new” arm is everyone who opted in to the beta, then the labels were never exchangeable in the first place. The permutation test will happily return a p-value that answers a question about randomization you never performed. This is Aug 31’s warning again: a clean computation on a self-selected comparison is still a self-selected comparison.

The general rule: shuffle only what the null says is interchangeable.

What shuffling builds

In a permutation test comparing two groups, you shuffle the group labels 10,000 times and recompute the difference in means each time.

What is the resulting distribution of 10,000 differences?

This is the idea worth taking away from the whole chapter, because it turns the null hypothesis from a sentence into a procedure you can watch run.

“There is no treatment effect” says that a subject’s outcome would have been the same whichever arm they had been assigned to. If that is true, then the labels are just tags stuck on afterwards, and any other assignment of the same tags to the same outcomes was equally likely to have happened. Shuffling reproduces exactly those alternative worlds, and the histogram of shuffled differences is what “no effect” looks like at your sample size, with your data’s spread and your group sizes.

The p-value is then a counting exercise with no formula in it: what fraction of those null worlds produced a difference at least as extreme as the one you actually got?

Note what it is not. It is not a sampling distribution of your estimate—it is centered at zero by construction, not at the observed difference, so you cannot read a confidence interval off it. That is the bootstrap’s job. The two procedures look similar on screen and answer opposite questions: the bootstrap resamples observations with replacement, keeping labels attached, and asks how much the estimate would move; the permutation test reassigns labels without replacement, keeping the observations fixed, and asks what would happen if the labels meant nothing.

Any statistic you like

Here is the payoff for the week. The engagement data is right-skewed and a couple of heavy users are pulling the means around, so suppose you would rather compare medians. There is no median t-test. There does not need to be:

Medians of 69 and 81, a gap of 12 minutes, p = 0.0157. One word changed in the code—mean became median—and the entire inferential apparatus followed.

The same substitution works for a difference of trimmed means, a ratio of medians, a difference in 90th percentiles, a difference in standard deviations, or a correlation. Choose the statistic that answers your question, then test it. That is the opposite of the usual order, in which people pick the question their software has a test for.

A/B tests, revisited

A/B testing was built on the two-proportion test. Permutation handles it with the same three lines, and the labels are literally the randomization.

1,200 visitors, split evenly between two versions of a landing page; the outcome is whether they clicked.

B converted 7.83% against A’s 6.33%—a lift of 1.5 percentage points, which is nearly a quarter more conversions and would be a genuinely valuable result. Is it real?

p = 0.365. Reshuffling which visitors were called “B” produces a gap of 1.5 points or more about a third of the time. There is no evidence here at all.

The two-proportion test says 0.368, agreeing to the third decimal.

The A/B testing lesson, in numbers

The experiment saw 38 clicks and 47 clicks. A difference of nine events is what the whole decision rests on, and nine events is nothing. This is what an underpowered A/B test looks like from the inside: a headline lift of 24%, a plausible business story, and a p-value of 0.37.

The failure was not in the analysis, it was in the sample-size calculation before the test was launched—and the temptation now, to keep the test running until the gap looks convincing, is precisely the peeking problem. The permutation test is not a way around any of that. It is a better-behaved way of getting the same disappointing answer.

Shuffling what you are not allowed to shuffle

Thirty patients each had their blood pressure measured before and after eight weeks on a drug. An analyst stacks the 60 measurements into one vector, labels each one "before" or "after", shuffles the 60 labels 10,000 times, and reports the resulting permutation p-value.

What is wrong with this?

Permutation tests are assumption-light, not assumption-free. The one assumption they do make is exchangeability under the null: if the null hypothesis is true, every relabelling you are willing to generate must have been equally likely to occur.

Free shuffling of all 60 labels asserts that patient 7’s “before” reading was just as likely to have been patient 22’s “after” reading. It was not. Patients differ enormously from each other in baseline blood pressure, and that between-patient variation is exactly what the paired design was built to remove. Throwing all 60 readings into one pot puts that variation back into the null distribution, which becomes far too wide—and the test loses most of its power.

The fix keeps the structure the design gave you. Under the null “the drug does nothing”, each patient’s two readings could equally well have arrived in either order, so you flip a coin within each pair, 30 independent flips, and recompute the mean difference. That is a valid permutation test, and it is the resampling counterpart of the paired t-test.

The general rule: shuffle only what the null hypothesis says is interchangeable. Repeated measures, matched pairs, students clustered in classrooms, and readings from the same sensor over time all constrain what you are allowed to permute—the same structure that broke Step 2 of the Week 5 derivation breaks free shuffling here, and for the same reason.

Bootstrap or permutation?

They look nearly identical on screen and answer different questions. Before you write the loop, decide which row you are in.

Bootstrap Permutation test
The question how much would my estimate move? could this have happened by chance?
Resample observations, with replacement labels, without replacement
What stays fixed which label each observation carries the observed values
Centered at your observed statistic zero
Output standard error, confidence interval p-value
Typical use “the median gift is $27 (95% CI $19–$41)” “the redesign’s 10.9-minute gain has p = 0.01”

A useful habit: report both. An interval says how big the effect might be; a p-value says whether you can rule out zero. The interval is almost always the more useful of the two in a report, and the p-value is what a reviewer will ask for anyway.

Exercises

Exercise: the observed statistic

mean(minutes[arm == "new"]) - mean(minutes[arm == "old"])

= 81.733 - 70.833 = 10.9.

Exercise: where is the null distribution centered?

```{r}
mean(replicate(5000, {
  s <- sample(arm)
  mean(minutes[s == "new"]) - mean(minutes[s == "old"])
}))
```

About 0. Under free shuffling neither label is favored, so the null distribution is centered at zero.

Exercise: compute a permutation p-value

Different data this time—a small randomized trial with 20 subjects per arm.

```{r}
observed <- mean(resp[group == "treated"]) - mean(resp[group == "control"])

perm <- replicate(10000, {
  s <- sample(group)
  mean(resp[s == "treated"]) - mean(resp[s == "control"])
})

mean(abs(perm) >= abs(observed))
```

About 0.047, against t.test()’s 0.0469.

Exercise: change the statistic, keep the machinery

```{r}
observed_med <- median(minutes[arm == "new"]) - median(minutes[arm == "old"])

perm_med <- replicate(10000, {
  s <- sample(arm)
  median(minutes[s == "new"]) - median(minutes[s == "old"])
})

mean(abs(perm_med) >= abs(observed_med))
```

About 0.0157.

Exercise: what does 0.010 license you to say?

The onboarding experiment gave an observed gap of 10.9 minutes and a
permutation p-value of 0.010: 100 of 10,000 label-shuffles produced a gap
at least that large in one direction or the other.

answer <- "C"

The p-value is a statement about data under an assumed null, not a probability attached to a hypothesis, and it is silent about effect size.

Summary

  • A permutation test turns the null hypothesis into a procedure: if the labels carry no information, every reassignment of them was equally likely, so reassign them and look.
  • sample(arm) shuffles labels without replacement, keeping the group sizes fixed and leaving the outcomes untouched.
  • The permutation distribution is centered at zero, by construction. It is what “no effect” looks like at your sample sizes and your spread.
  • The p-value is a tail proportion: 100 of 10,000 shuffles reached 10.9 minutes or more in either direction, so p = 0.010. No formula, no degrees of freedom.
  • On the same data the Welch t-test gave p = 0.0072. Agreement where both apply is what licenses the method where only one does.
  • The one assumption is exchangeability under the null, and it is free in a randomized experiment. It fails for paired data, clustered data, and non-random assignment—shuffle only what the null says is interchangeable.
  • The statistic is yours to choose. Swapping mean for median gave p = 0.0157 for a comparison with no textbook test at all.
  • A/B tests are permutation tests. The landing-page experiment showed a 24% relative lift, nine extra clicks, and p = 0.365—which is what an underpowered test looks like from the inside, and no analysis method fixes it.
  • Bootstrap for intervals, permutation for p-values. With replacement and labels fixed, or without replacement and labels shuffled. Decide which before writing the loop.
