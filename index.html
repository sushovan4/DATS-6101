Course Description

This course introduces the concepts and practice of data science: framing a research question that data can answer, preparing and exploring real data, determining what the evidence supports, and communicating the result. Topics progress from exploratory analysis through probability and statistical inference to linear and logistic modeling, where the emphasis is on what a fitted model licenses you to claim.

R is used throughout, with RStudio, RMarkdown, and Git. Every code example in the course notes runs in the browser, with no installation needed to try it.

Students are expected to use AI assistants and to identify when those assistants are wrong. Assignments are graded on that judgment as well as on correctness; see Use of AI Assistants.

Learning Outcomes

As a result of completing this course, students will be able to:

  1. Frame a research question on real data using S.M.A.R.T. principles, and say what evidence would answer it;
  2. Import, clean, and reshape messy real-world data in R;
  3. Explore data and describe it honestly, choosing summaries and visualizations that do not mislead;
  4. Reason with random variables: compute and interpret expectation and variance, apply conditional probability and Bayes’ rule, and explain why variances of independent quantities add;
  5. Derive and use the fact that the standard error of a mean is \sigma/\sqrt{n}, and reason about sampling distributions and the central limit theorem by simulation rather than taking them on faith;
  6. Construct and interpret confidence intervals, and state precisely what they do and do not claim;
  7. Choose and carry out an appropriate hypothesis test, interpret a p-value without overstating it, compute the power of a study and say what a non-significant result is worth, and recognize when multiple comparisons have inflated a finding;
  8. Design and analyze a randomized experiment end to end—assignment, sample size, guardrail metrics and a stopping rule fixed in advance—and explain what randomization licenses that an observational comparison does not;
  9. Judge whether a sample can answer the question asked of it, identifying selection and survivorship bias and reasoning about why data is missing rather than only removing it;
  10. Fit a linear model and treat each coefficient as an estimate: report its standard error and confidence interval, test it, and state precisely what “holding the other variables constant” does and does not buy;
  11. Check a fitted model rather than trusting it—read residual plots, identify influential observations, and explain how confounding and omitted variables change a conclusion, and state what would be needed to license a causal claim without an experiment;
  12. Distinguish a confidence interval from a prediction interval, and use resampling—the bootstrap and permutation tests—to quantify uncertainty when the standard formulas do not apply;
  13. Fit and interpret a logistic model, report odds ratios with intervals, and explain why linear regression will not do for a binary outcome;
  14. Collaborate with teammates using Git, and defend a shared analysis out loud to both technical and general audiences.

Course Prerequisites and Software

You are expected to have a basic understanding of introductory statistics and some exposure to R, whether through prior coursework or online resources. This course does not teach programming from zero.

No class session is spent on R itself. The language is instead covered by the R Primer, a self-paced part of the notes that runs alongside the course from day one: the environment and RStudio, R’s core objects, coercion and missing values, factors and data frames, plotting with ggplot2, reproducible documents, and Git, with browser-based exercises throughout that check themselves and say which misconception you hit. Work through it in the opening weeks and use office hours for anything that will not come right.

The GW bootcamp’s Programming track covers the habits behind it—Git, debugging, and environments—but it is taught in Python, so the R itself is what the Primer is for. The bootcamp’s Math track is the foundation for DATS 6103 rather than for this course.

Install the following before Aug 24:

  1. R—https://www.r-project.org/
  2. RStudio—https://posit.co/products/open-source/rstudio/
  3. Git, and a free GitHub account—https://github.com/
  4. Access to an AI assistant (ChatGPT, Claude, Copilot, Gemini, or similar). Free tiers are sufficient; no paid subscription is required.

The Data Science Help Desk exists for exactly this: it is staffed for students in the program or taking DATS classes, in Samson Hall 304—the same stairs as my office, one floor up—and dshelpdesk@gwu.edu will arrange a remote session. Take a setup problem there in the first two weeks rather than to the week an assignment is due.

Course Format

This course is taught as a flipped workshop. Lecture material is delivered as assigned reading; class time is spent on discussion and supervised lab work. Slides are not presented in class, and content is not re-covered from the front of the room. Reading before each session is therefore required.

Each session runs in three modes:

  • Question triage. Instructor-led, drawn entirely from the questions students posted on the reading.
  • Reasoning and discussion. Students predict outputs, select tests, and defend conclusions to one another.
  • Lab. Supervised hands-on work at the keyboard.

Before class: readings and questions

Each class day has an assigned reading, listed in the course schedule. Every chapter of the course notes carries a comment box; students post questions there as they read, and may also answer one another. Questions are due Monday at 12:00 noon, the morning of class. One substantive question earns the week’s credit—a question that identifies what the reading left unclear, rather than a request to restate it. The two lowest weeks are dropped.

The first class is an exception: no reading is due before Aug 24. Students should arrive with R and RStudio installed. Questions and Data is read after that session; assigned reading proper begins with Exploratory Data Analysis, due before Aug 31, with the first questions due Monday, Aug 31 at noon.

The R Primer sits outside this cycle. It is not tied to any session and has no question deadline; post there whenever something does not come right, and those questions are picked up in office hours or at the start of a session.

The first session is ungraded

No work on Aug 24 counts toward the final grade. It is for installing software, registering with the comment system, and becoming familiar with the format.

In-class work and how it is graded

In-class work is assessed on observed participation rather than on submitted files. Each session is worth three points:

Component Criterion
Interview Took both roles in the discussion, confirmed by a signed rubric card from whoever interviewed you.
Discussion Contributed substantively at the checkpoint.
Build Engaged with the lab and able to describe the current difficulty when asked.

None of the three criteria requires a correct answer. The two lowest sessions are dropped, without documentation or prior permission.

Participation is recorded at eleven sessions—every meeting except Aug 24, which is ungraded, Oct 26, which is the midterm, and Dec 10, which is the presentation session. No points are recorded on those three days, and there is no class on Nov 16. With the two lowest dropped, nine sessions count.

Students work in a pair for the discussion and a group of three or four for the lab. When an odd number is present, one discussion group is a trio and the interview goes round it—A interviews B, B interviews C, C interviews A—so everyone still takes both roles. Three roles rotate within each lab group: the driver types, the navigator reads documentation and does not type, and the skeptic is responsible for identifying what would falsify each result. In a group of four, two share the skeptic’s job.

Homework

Homework is individual and due most weeks, Sunday at 11:59 p.m. Each assignment is graded on the correctness of the analysis and on the quality of the AI disclosure described under Use of AI Assistants. Details are posted on Blackboard. Every homework counts—no homework score is dropped.

Project

A team project takes a real dataset through the full life cycle: S.M.A.R.T. questions, acquisition, cleaning, exploration, modeling, and a written and spoken account of the findings.

Deliverables are a GitHub repository, a written report stating what the analysis does and does not support, an AI disclosure statement, and a presentation. Presentation questions are answered individually, so members of one team may receive different grades.

The project is proposed Oct 19 and presented on Thursday, December 10. The start time is announced during the term.

Midterm Exam

The midterm is held in class on Monday, October 26 and covers the material through Oct 19: the data science life cycle, exploratory analysis, probability, random variables, expectation and variance, the central limit theorem, interval estimation, hypothesis testing, and the choice of an appropriate test.

The exam is individual and taken without AI assistance. As with in-class work, this does not mean from memory; permitted materials will be announced in advance.

Late and Missed Work

Homework may be submitted up to 48 hours late, twice in the term, with no penalty and no reason required. Say in the submission that you are using one. Beyond those two, and beyond 48 hours, homework is not accepted and scores zero. All eight assignments count, which is why the two extensions exist: there is no dropped homework score behind them.

No documentation or prior permission is needed to use an extension. In-class work and reading questions are the two components that do carry drops—the two lowest of each—and those cover illness, travel and everything else on the same terms.

The midterm and the project presentation are fixed dates and cannot be dropped. A make-up is arranged only for a documented emergency, and only if you contact me as early as you are able.

Homework submitted with AI assistance must include the prompt log and a short critique identifying what the assistant got wrong or would have got wrong if accepted unchanged. The critique is graded alongside the analysis.

In-class work is unaided with respect to AI. It is not closed-book: the R documentation and these course notes may be consulted freely.

Submitting a fabricated or altered prompt log constitutes falsification of data under the Academic Integrity Code and will be reported. Declaring that no AI was used is always an acceptable answer.

This policy differs from other sections

Other sections of DATS 6101 may set a different AI policy. Students are bound by the policy of the section in which they are enrolled. The policies for this section and for DATS 6103 with this instructor are identical.

Academic Integrity Code

Academic integrity is an essential part of the educational process, and all members of the GW community take these matters very seriously. As the instructor of record for this course, my role is to provide clear expectations and uphold them in all assessments. Violations of academic integrity occur when students fail to cite research sources properly, engage in unauthorized collaboration, falsify data, use resources not permitted for a given assessment, and otherwise violate the Code of Academic Integrity. If you have any questions about whether particular academic practices or resources are permitted, you should ask me for clarification.
