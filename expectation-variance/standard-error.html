The Standard Error of a Mean


37 minutes

This is the chapter the course is built on.

You have a sample. You computed its mean, and R printed a number to seven figures with complete confidence. But that number came from the particular rows that happened to end up in your file, and a different sample would have given a different answer. Describing a Single Variable ended on exactly this discomfort, and Expectation sharpened it: six samples from the same distribution gave six different means.

The question is: how far off is \bar{x} likely to be?

It sounds like an impossible question. You have one sample. You cannot see the others. And yet the answer takes four lines of algebra, uses nothing beyond the two rules you just met, and is the reason every confidence interval, every p-value, and every standard error printed beside a regression coefficient exists.

The set-up

Take n observations X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n, drawn independently from a population with expectation \mu and standard deviation \sigma. The sample mean is

\bar{X} = \frac{1}{n}(X_1 + X_2 + \cdots + X_n)

Notice the capital letter. \bar{X} is written as a random variable, because before you collect the data it is one—it has a distribution, with an expectation and a variance of its own. That distribution has a name, the sampling distribution of the mean, and everything below is about it.

First, where is it centered?

By linearity from Expectation, and remembering that linearity asks for nothing at all:

E[\bar X] = \frac{1}{n}\bigl(E[X_1] + \cdots + E[X_n]\bigr) = \frac{1}{n}\cdot n\mu = \mu

The sample mean is centered on the truth. It is not systematically too big or too small. In the language of Describing a Single Variable, it is unbiased.

That is reassuring and it is not enough. Unbiased says the errors average out over infinitely many repetitions; it says nothing about the size of the error in the one sample you actually have. For that we need the variance.

Now, how much does it move?

The derivation

\operatorname{Var}(\bar X) \;=\; \operatorname{Var}\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\right)

Step 1—pull the constant out. By the scaling rule \operatorname{Var}(aX) = a^2\operatorname{Var}(X) from Variance and Standard Deviation, with a = 1/n:

= \frac{1}{n^2}\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\right)

Step 2—split the sum. The observations are independent, so variances add:

= \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^n \operatorname{Var}(X_i)

Step 3—every term is the same. Each X_i comes from the same population, so each has variance \sigma^2, and there are n of them:

= \frac{1}{n^2}\cdot n\sigma^2 \;=\; \frac{\sigma^2}{n}

Step 4—square root, to get back to the data’s units:

\operatorname{SD}(\bar X) \;=\; \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}

This is the standard error of the mean, written \operatorname{SE}(\bar X).

Four lines. Look at what each one used:

Step What it needed
E[\bar X] = \mu linearity—no assumptions
Step 1 \operatorname{Var}(aX) = a^2\operatorname{Var}(X)—no assumptions
Step 2 variances add—requires independence
Step 3 identical distribution

Exactly one step needed an assumption about how the observations relate to each other, and it is Step 2. That is why 5.2 spent so long breaking the addition rule on purpose. If your observations are not independent—repeated measures on the same patient, students clustered in classrooms, daily readings from a sensor that drifts—Step 2 is wrong, the covariance terms are usually positive, and \sigma/\sqrt{n} is an underestimate of the real wobble. Software will print it anyway. It has no way of knowing.

Notice too where the \sqrt{\cdot} comes from. There is no square root anywhere in the derivation until the last line, and its only job is to convert squared units back into readable ones. The n divides the variance; the \sqrt{n} is what that looks like after the units are fixed.

Watching it happen

Algebra is a claim. Here is the check.

We will pretend to be a population with \mu = 100 and \sigma = 12. For each sample size n, we draw 5,000 separate samples, compute the mean of each, and then measure how spread out those 5,000 means are. If the derivation is right, that spread should come out at 12/\sqrt{n}.

n 4 16 64 256
observed 5.9903 2.9748 1.4892 0.7592
predicted 12/\sqrt{n} 6.0000 3.0000 1.5000 0.7500

Within about 1% at every sample size, and the residual gap is simulation noise—5,000 replications is a lot but it is not infinity. Nothing was fitted. The predicted row was computed from \sigma and n alone, without looking at the simulation.

Plot it on log-log axes, where \sigma/\sqrt{n} becomes a straight line of slope -1/2:

What the sampling distribution looks like

The same 5,000-sample experiment, drawn, at the smallest and largest n:

Both are centered on 100—that is E[\bar X] = \mu, visible. But at n = 4 the sample means ranged from 79.18 to 123.57, and at n = 256 from 97.25 to 102.65. Same population, same estimator, and one of those two pictures would let you report a useful number while the other would not.

This is the diagram that becomes a confidence interval. Once you know how wide the sampling distribution is, and roughly what shape it has, you can say how far from \mu your one sample mean plausibly landed—and run the statement backwards to get an interval around \bar{x}.

It does not need a Normal population

A reasonable worry: all of that used rnorm, so perhaps \sigma/\sqrt{n} is a fact about Normal distributions.

It is not. Nothing in the derivation mentioned the shape of the distribution—only its variance. Here is the same experiment on a heavily right-skewed exponential population, rate 0.4, whose standard deviation is 1/0.4 = 2.5:

1.2697, 0.6292, 0.3147, 0.1549 against 1.25, 0.625, 0.3125, 0.15625. The rule holds for a distribution that looks nothing like a bell curve.

What does depend on the population shape is the shape of the sampling distribution, not its width. That is the central limit theorem, and it is next week’s chapter.

What 1/\sqrt{n} costs

The derivation is done. Now the consequence, which is the part that will follow you into every job you have.

Each column has four times the data of the one before it, and each SE is half the one before it: 1.20, 0.60, 0.30, 0.15.

The rule to carry around

To halve the standard error, quadruple the sample. To divide it by k, multiply the sample by k^2.

Data collection typically costs roughly linearly in n. Precision improves like \sqrt{n}. So every additional decimal place of precision costs a hundred times what the previous one did.

This is not an abstraction. It is the number that decides whether a proposal is a week of work or a year of it:

To pin the mean down to \pm 0.25 you need 2,304 observations; to get to \pm 0.5, only 576. The last halving cost you 1,728 extra observations—three times everything you had collected up to that point.

Two practical readings of the same fact:

  • Early data is cheap and valuable. Going from 10 observations to 40 is transformative. This is why a pilot study is worth running.
  • Late data is expensive and marginal. Going from 10,000 to 40,000 buys you the same factor of improvement, at 3,000 times the cost of the first jump. When someone asks for “just a bit more precision” at the end of a project, this is the arithmetic you show them.

And the one that matters most for reading other people’s work: an enormous sample makes the standard error tiny, and does nothing whatever about bias. A survey of two million people who all opted in has a beautiful standard error and may be wrong by twenty points. \sigma/\sqrt{n} measures the wobble from sampling, and only that. It has no opinion about whether you sampled the right people.

Quadrupling the sample

Your current study has n = 400 and a standard error of 3.0. Your manager wants the standard error down to 1.0, and asks how much more data that will take.

\text{SE} = \sigma/\sqrt{n}, so the SE depends on n only through \sqrt{n}. To divide the SE by k you must multiply n by k^2. Here k = 3, so n goes from 400 to 9 \times 400 = 3{,}600.

Check it directly: \text{SE} = 3.0 at n = 400 means \sigma = 3\sqrt{400} = 60, and 60/\sqrt{3600} = 60/60 = 1.0.

The special case worth memorizing is k = 2: quadrupling the sample halves the error. One quadrupling takes 400 to 1,600 and the SE from 3.0 to 1.5—not to 1.0, which is why the third option falls short.

The reason this matters outside the arithmetic is that data collection usually costs roughly linearly in n while precision improves like \sqrt{n}. Every further decimal place costs a hundred times what the last one did. When someone asks for “a bit more precision” at the end of a project, this is the number that tells you whether they are asking for a week of work or a year of it.

The proportion is a mean in disguise

Before leaving this, the special case you will use more than any other.

A conversion rate, a click rate, a defect rate, a survey’s “yes” share—each is a proportion, and a proportion is the mean of a column of zeros and ones. So everything above applies with nothing changed.

Write X_i = 1 if the ith visitor converts and 0 otherwise. Then \hat p = \bar X, and a Bernoulli variable has variance p(1-p), so the derivation you just did hands you the answer:

\operatorname{SE}(\hat p) = \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}

No new theorem. The same \sigma/\sqrt{n}, with \sigma = \sqrt{p(1-p)} substituted.

The last two agree to four decimals, which is the point: sd(visits)/sqrt(n) and \sqrt{p(1-p)/n} are the same calculation.

Two consequences worth carrying.

The spread depends on the rate. p(1-p) is largest at p = 0.5 and shrinks towards either end, so a rate near 50% is the hardest to pin down and a rate near 1% is comparatively easy in absolute terms—but not in relative terms, which is what anyone actually cares about:

A 3% conversion rate measured on 4,000 visitors carries a standard error of 0.0027—which sounds tiny until you notice it is 9% of the thing being measured. That is why rare-event experiments need so much traffic, and it is the arithmetic behind every “we need to run this test for another three weeks”.

You need p to plan for p. Rearranging for n requires the rate you have not measured yet, so in practice you either use a pilot estimate or take the worst case p = 0.5, which gives the largest n and cannot be an underestimate.

The SD of the data versus the SE of the mean

Now the confusion that outlives every course. It is worth slowing down for, because it is asked in interviews, it appears in published papers, and the two numbers can differ by a factor of thirty in the same analysis.

The mean is 100.2270. The SD is 12.2321. The SE is 0.6116. Twenty times apart, computed from the same 400 numbers, and both are correct—they answer different questions.

Two numbers, two questions
SD of the data, s SE of the mean, s/\sqrt{n}
Answers how much do individuals differ? how much would my estimate move on a re-run?
Describes the population the estimate
As n grows settles down to \sigma—does not shrink shrinks like 1/\sqrt{n}—goes to zero
Goes in the sentence “a typical adult is within about 12 of 100” “the population mean is 100.2, give or take 0.6”

People do not become more alike because you measured more of them. Your estimate of their average does get sharper.

Watch both behave, at four sample sizes from the same population:

n 25 100 400 1600
SD of the data 12.94 12.06 11.88 12.04
SE of the mean 2.59 1.21 0.59 0.30

The top row wanders around 12 and stays there. The bottom row falls by half every time n quadruples. Different rows, different jobs.

Where this goes wrong in the wild
  • Error bars on a chart with no caption saying which they are. SE bars are about \sqrt{n} times shorter than SD bars, so the same data can be drawn to look tight or scattered depending on a choice the reader cannot see. If a figure does not say, it does not mean anything. In your own work, say.
  • “±” in a report. “Mean satisfaction 7.2 ± 1.8” is ambiguous and the two readings support entirely different conclusions.
  • Dividing by n instead of \sqrt{n}. It produces a number small enough to look impressive and it is simply wrong. Check the units of your own claim: if you would not believe the precision, you probably made this mistake.
  • The n that goes under the root is the number of independent observations. Fifty measurements on ten patients is not n = 50. Step 2 of the derivation is where that breaks.
Which number goes in the sentence?

A survey of 900 adults reports a mean daily screen time of 4.2 hours with a standard deviation of 2.4 hours.

You are writing the sentence that says how precisely the population mean has been pinned down. Which number belongs in that sentence?

This is the most common confusion in applied statistics, and it turns up in interviews for exactly that reason.

The standard deviation of the data, s = 2.4 hours, answers: how spread out are people? It is an estimate of a fixed feature of the population. Survey 90,000 adults instead of 900 and it stays at roughly 2.4—people do not become more alike because you measured more of them.

The standard error of the mean, s/\sqrt{n} = 0.08 hours, answers: how much would my headline number wobble if I redid the whole survey? It is a property of the estimate, not of the population, and it shrinks as 1/\sqrt{n}.

So: “a typical adult is within about 2.4 hours of 4.2” uses the SD. “The population mean is pinned down to about \pm 0.16 hours” uses the SE. Swap them and you either claim an absurd precision about individuals or an absurd imprecision about your estimate.

Dividing by n rather than \sqrt{n} is the third error, and it is not a small one: 0.0027 hours is about ten seconds, which would be an extraordinary claim from 900 self-reported diary entries. The \sqrt{\cdot} is not decoration; it falls out of the derivation \operatorname{Var}(\bar X) = \sigma^2/n.

  sample mean = 31.4 minutes
  sd of the data = 14.0 minutes

answer <- "B"

The question is about variation among residents, which is the SD of the data. The SE answers a question about the precision of the estimate, which nobody asked.

Where this goes

You now have the result the rest of the course quotes:

\operatorname{SE}(\bar X) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}, \qquad \text{estimated in practice by } \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}

In real work you do not know \sigma, so you substitute the sample standard deviation s. That substitution introduces extra uncertainty of its own, which is small when n is large and not small when n is small—and it is exactly why the t distribution exists, with its n-1 degrees of freedom, the same n-1 Describing a Single Variable argued about.

  • Confidence intervals. You know the sampling distribution is centered at \mu with width \sigma/\sqrt{n}; the central limit theorem will tell you it is roughly Normal. Put those together and \bar{x} \pm 2\,\text{SE} becomes an interval with a stateable success rate. There is nothing else in it.
  • Hypothesis tests. A test statistic is (\text{estimate} - \text{claim})/\text{SE}—“how many standard errors away is what I saw from what you claimed?” The SE is the denominator of every test in this course.
  • A/B testing. How long to run the test is the sample-size calculation you just did, with a difference of two means in place of one mean.
  • Regression. Every coefficient in every model output comes with a standard error beside it, and it means precisely what it means here: how much would this number move if I collected the data again.

If you take one thing from The Standard Error of a Mean, take this: every “give or take” in statistics is a standard error, and every standard error is a variance divided by n with a square root taken at the end.

Summary

  • E[\bar X] = \mu: the sample mean is centered on the truth, by linearity, with no assumptions required.
  • \operatorname{Var}(\bar X) = \sigma^2/n, and so \operatorname{SE}(\bar X) = \sigma/\sqrt{n}. Four lines, using only the scaling rule and the addition rule.
  • Independence is used exactly once, in the step where variances add. When observations are clustered or repeated, that step fails, the covariances are usually positive, and the reported SE is too small.
  • Simulation confirms it: at n = 4, 16, 64, 256 the spread of 5,000 sample means came out 5.99, 2.97, 1.49, 0.76 against a predicted 6, 3, 1.5, 0.75. It works for skewed populations too—the derivation never mentioned shape.
  • Quadruple the sample to halve the error. n = (\sigma/\text{SE})^2. Early data is cheap and valuable; late data is expensive and marginal.
  • A tiny standard error says nothing about bias. Two million self-selected respondents give a beautiful SE and can still be badly wrong.
  • SD of the data describes people and does not shrink with n. SE of the mean describes your estimate and shrinks like 1/\sqrt{n}. Say which one your error bars are.
  • A proportion is a mean of zeros and ones, so \operatorname{SE}(\hat p) = \sqrt{p(1-p)/n} with no new theory. Rare rates have small absolute errors and large relative ones, which is why they need so much traffic.
