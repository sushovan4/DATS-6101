Expectation


27 minutes

Last week you met random variables and the distributions we actually use. A distribution is a complete description of a random quantity: every value it can take and how often. That is more information than anyone can hold in their head, so we do to distributions exactly what Describing a Single Variable did to columns of data—replace them with a couple of numbers.

The first of those numbers is the expectation, E[X]. It is the center of the distribution in the same sense the mean was the center of a dataset: the balance point.

But the definition worth carrying around is the operational one.

What E[X] actually means

E[X] is the number the average of your observations settles down to as you collect more and more of them.

Not “the value you expect to see”—for a fair die E[X] = 3.5, which is not a face. Not “the most likely value”. It is the long-run average, and everything else in this chapter follows from taking that seriously.

Watching it settle

The cleanest way to believe this is to watch it happen. Roll a fair die ten thousand times and track the running average after every roll.

After 10 rolls the running mean is 3.30. After 100 it is 3.51, after 1,000 it is 3.556, and after 10,000 it is 3.4909. Now look at the whole path, with E[X] = 3.5 drawn as a dashed line. The horizontal axis is on a log scale, because otherwise the first thousand rolls are squeezed into an invisible sliver at the left:

Two things are worth noticing, and the second one is the one people miss.

It settles. The early wobbling is violent—after five rolls the running mean can easily be 2 or 5—and then it calms down and hugs the line. That convergence has a name, the law of large numbers, and it is the reason a casino with enough customers has no risk at all while any individual customer has plenty.

It settles slowly. Look at the axis: it took a log scale to make the picture readable, because the improvement from 100 rolls to 1,000 rolls looks about the same as the improvement from 1,000 to 10,000. Getting one more decimal place costs a hundred times the data. You have just seen 1/\sqrt{n} without being told about it, and in The Standard Error of a Mean we will pin the rate down exactly.

Computing E[X]

The discrete case

For a discrete random variable, weight each value by its probability and add:

E[X] = \sum_{x} x \cdot P(X = x)

That is a weighted average, and the weights are the probabilities. Here is a scratch card. It costs $2, and the prize table is:

Prize $0 $1 $5 $20
Probability 0.70 0.20 0.08 0.02

The expected prize is exactly $1.00. The card costs $2. So the expected profit per card, from your side, is -\$1.00: every card you buy costs you a dollar on average, forever, regardless of how you feel about the one that paid $20.

Believe the arithmetic, then check it anyway:

Half a million cards give a mean prize of 0.9967 and a mean profit of -1.0033. The formula and the simulation are computing the same thing by two routes, which is exactly the habit this week is trying to build: when a formula surprises you, simulate it.

Note the scale at which the expectation becomes a fact rather than a tendency. Sell 100,000 of these cards and the operator’s take is close to $100,000 with very little uncertainty. Buy one, and the expectation tells you almost nothing about your evening. Same number, two completely different kinds of usefulness.

The continuous case

For a continuous random variable the sum becomes an integral, weighted by the density f(x) instead of by point probabilities:

E[X] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x \, f(x)\,dx

You will not be asked to evaluate integrals in this course. What you do need is that it is the same idea—every possible value, weighted by how much probability sits near it—and that for the distributions you met last week the answer is already known:

Distribution E[X]
Bernoulli(p) p
Binomial(n, p) np
Normal(\mu, \sigma) \mu
Uniform(a, b) (a+b)/2
Exponential(rate \lambda) 1/\lambda
Poisson(\lambda) \lambda

Check one of them. An exponential with rate 0.4 should have expectation 1/0.4 = 2.5:

2.5029 against 2.5. Close, and not exact—which is the subject of the next section.

E[X] is not \bar{x}

This distinction is worth more to you than any formula in the chapter, and it is the one that quietly confuses people for years.

Draw 30 observations from that same exponential, six separate times, and compute the sample mean each time:

2.869, 3.329, 2.356, 3.123, 2.298, 2.147. Six numbers, all of them the “average” of an exponential with expectation 2.5, none of them 2.5, and the spread between the largest and the smallest is over a full unit.

Two objects, two kinds
E[X], or \mu \bar{x}
What it belongs to the distribution a sample
Is it random? no—it is a fixed number yes—a different sample gives a different value
Do you know it? usually not always, you computed it
How do you get it? from the model, by formula from data, with mean()

\bar{x} is an estimate of \mu. It is right on average and wrong in every particular sample. The question “how wrong, typically?” is not answerable from anything in this chapter—it needs the variance, which is 5.2, and then the derivation in The Standard Error of a Mean.

Notation helps and is worth adopting now: Greek letters (\mu, \sigma) for population quantities you do not know, Latin letters (\bar{x}, s) for things you computed from data. When you see a formula with both in it, it is telling you something about how the second relates to the first.

Expectation versus sample mean

You simulate 30 draws from an exponential distribution whose expectation is exactly 2.5, and mean() of your draws returns 2.869.

What is the relationship between the 2.5 and the 2.869?

These are two different kinds of object and keeping them apart is most of what this week is for.

E[X] is a number attached to the distribution. It is not random, it does not depend on any data, and no amount of sampling changes it. For an exponential with rate 0.4 it is 1/0.4 = 2.5, full stop.

\bar{x} is a number attached to a sample. It is computed from data, and because the data are random, \bar{x} is itself a random quantity. Run the same simulation six times and you get six different values—in the chapter, 2.869, 3.329, 2.356, 3.123, 2.298, 2.147.

Nothing is broken. \bar{x} is an estimate of E[X]: correct on average (that is what E[\bar{X}] = \mu says) and wrong in every individual sample. The question the rest of the course answers is “wrong by how much, typically?”—and the answer, derived in 5.3, is \sigma/\sqrt{n}.

Notation is a real help here. Greek letters (\mu, \sigma) are population quantities you do not know; Latin letters with bars and hats (\bar{x}, s) are things you computed from data.

Exercise: what is a fair price?

sum(prize * p)

The expected prize is $1.00, so $1.00 is the fair price. Every cent above that is the operator’s expected margin per card.

Linearity

Now the property that makes expectation genuinely useful, as opposed to merely meaningful.

Constants pass straight through

E[aX + b] = a\,E[X] + b

If you double every value and add ten, the balance point doubles and moves by ten. This is not deep; it is what “balance point” means. But it is worth verifying once so you trust it later:

mean(x) is 4.9992, and both routes to E[3X+10] give 24.9976. Identical, not approximately identical, because the rule holds for the sample mean exactly as it holds for the expectation.

The practical use is unit conversion. If waiting times average 14 minutes, then in seconds they average 60 \times 14 = 840, and nobody has to re-derive anything.

Sums add, and independence is not required

E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y]

This one deserves your attention. It holds always. It does not care whether X and Y are independent, correlated, or one is a deterministic function of the other.

Most people, having been told all term that independence matters, assume it must be lurking here. It is not. Watch: let Y be X^2, which is about as dependent on X as anything can be.

E[X] \approx 4.999, E[Y] \approx 29.009, and both the mean of the sum and the sum of the means come out at 34.0079. To the last printed digit.

Now the contrast that shows independence does matter somewhere. Try the same thing with a product:

185.20 against 145.02. Not close, and not noise—E[XY] = E[X]E[Y] is a different rule and it genuinely requires independence, which X and X^2 spectacularly lack.

The line to remember

Sums are free. Products are not.

E[X+Y] = E[X]+E[Y] needs no assumptions at all. E[XY] = E[X]E[Y] needs independence.

This is why you can compute the expected value of a total—revenue across regions, total delay across the legs of a journey, the sum of n observations in a sample—without knowing anything whatever about how the pieces move together. In 5.3 that is precisely what lets us find E[\bar X] in one line.

And then in Variance and Standard Deviation, variance will turn out to behave like the product rule, not the sum rule. That asymmetry is the whole reason independence is a condition worth checking.

Sums versus products

Let X be tomorrow’s high temperature and Y the number of ice creams a seaside kiosk sells tomorrow. These two are about as dependent as two variables get.

Which of the following are you entitled to write down?

Sums are free. Products are not.

E[X+Y] = E[X] + E[Y] is unconditional. There is no independence assumption anywhere in it, and this is genuinely surprising the first time you meet it—the average of a total is the total of the averages, no matter how tangled the two variables are. Averaging simply does not notice how the two variables move together.

E[XY] = E[X]E[Y] is a completely different statement, and it does require independence. On a hot day both X and Y are large at the same time, so the product is large exactly when both factors are, and the average product comes out well above the product of the averages.

The practical consequence: you can compute the expected value of any sum or any weighted total—revenue across regions, total delay across legs of a journey, the mean of a sample—without knowing anything at all about how the pieces are related. That is what makes the derivation in 5.3 possible. Variances are the ones that will demand independence, and that is exactly where 5.2 gets careful.

Exercise: linearity without re-simulating

1.8 * mean(x) + 32

mean(x) is 4.99919, so the answer is 1.8 \times 4.99919 + 32 = 40.99855. Try mean(1.8 * x + 32) afterwards and confirm it agrees to every printed digit.

Exercise: a sum of dependent variables

mean(u + v)

It returns 95.0036. By linearity E[V] = 100 - 2E[U] = 90, so E[U+V] = 95, and the dependence between U and V is irrelevant to the calculation.

A worked fair-game problem

Expectation questions in interviews are almost always one of these two shapes, and both are the same shape underneath.

Shape one: is this bet worth taking? A friend offers: roll a fair die, they pay you the face value in dollars, and you pay $4 to play.

E[X] = 3.5 for a fair die, so the expected profit is 3.5 - 4 = -0.50 per roll. Decline.

-0.5027 over 200,000 rolls, against the exact -0.50.

Shape two: what is the edge? American roulette, straight-up bet on a single number. There are 38 pockets. You stake $1; if your number comes up you win $35 in profit, otherwise you lose your $1.

-0.052632 per dollar staked. That is the famous 5.26% house edge, and it is the entire business model: a number small enough that no individual player notices it and large enough that, multiplied by the law of large numbers you watched in the first figure, it builds a building.

Notice the structure both problems share. List the outcomes, attach probabilities, multiply, sum, compare to the price. Insurance premiums, warranty pricing, the value of a coupon campaign, and whether to run an expensive experiment are all the same computation wearing different clothes.

Summary

  • E[X] is the number a running average settles down to. Watch it settle, and notice that it settles slowly—that slowness is 1/\sqrt{n}, and it is what The Standard Error of a Mean is about.
  • Discrete: E[X] = \sum x P(X=x), a weighted average with probabilities as weights. Continuous: the same idea with an integral. For the standard distributions the answer is a known formula.
  • E[X] is not \bar{x}. The first is a fixed property of a distribution; the second is a random quantity computed from one sample. Six samples gave six different means and none of them was the truth.
  • E[aX+b] = aE[X]+b. Constants pass straight through, which is why unit conversion is free.
  • E[X+Y] = E[X]+E[Y] always, independent or not. E[XY] = E[X]E[Y] requires independence. Sums are free; products are not.
  • Fair value equals expectation. Every “should I take this bet” problem is: list outcomes, weight by probability, sum, compare with the price.

Next: the second summary number. If the expectation says where a distribution sits, the variance says how far it typically strays—and it is the variance, not the expectation, that will make independence matter.
