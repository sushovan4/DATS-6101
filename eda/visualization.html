Visualization That Does Not Mislead


33 minutes

There is a comfortable way to think about this topic: there are honest charts and dishonest charts, and if you avoid a short list of tricks—truncated axes, 3D pie charts, misleading pictograms—you are on the right side of the line.

That story is wrong, and believing it is what makes people easy to fool.

Every chart is the result of decisions: what to put on each axis, how far the axes run, how wide the bins are, what to aggregate over, what to leave out. Each decision changes what a reader sees. There is no neutral setting. A chart is not a photograph of the data; it is a sentence about the data, and like any sentence it asserts something and can be argued with.

So the question to ask of your own figures is not “did I break a rule.” It is:

What claim does someone take away from this picture, and is that claim one I would be willing to write down and defend in words?

That reframing does more work than any checklist, because it applies to the choices no checklist anticipated.

Truncated axes

The canonical example, and worth doing properly. Two ballot measures:

Same two numbers. On the left, a near-tie. On the right, a rout.

The reason is arithmetic, and you can put a number on it. A bar chart invites the reader to compare lengths, so the ratio of drawn heights is what gets read as the ratio of quantities:

Plan A got 5% more votes than Plan B. The right-hand chart draws its bar 2.3 times as tall. Nothing in the figure is factually false—the axis is labeled, the values are correct, a careful reader could work it out. But “technically defensible under scrutiny” is a low bar, and the whole point of a chart is that people read it without scrutiny.

Bars from zero. Lines need not be.

Bars encode length, so the baseline is part of the encoding and it has to be zero. Truncating a bar chart’s axis breaks the visual metaphor it is built on.

Lines encode change, and forcing a line chart to include zero can be its own distortion—body temperature over a day, plotted from 0°C, is a flat line carrying no information. Choose a range that shows the variation that matters and say what the range is.

The general principle underneath: match the axis to what the reader is being asked to compare. Comparing magnitudes needs a zero. Comparing movements does not.

Exercise: how much does the truncation exaggerate?

(74 - 70) / (71 - 70)

The drawn heights are 4 and 1. A three-point move on a hundred-point scale becomes a fourfold visual difference.

Aspect ratio

Less famous than the truncated axis, and it distorts just as effectively—with the added convenience that nobody can point to anything wrong.

Here is the same enrollment series, same data, same axis limits, drawn in two panels of different widths:

The left panel shows a steep climb. The right panel shows a gentle drift upward. Identical numbers, identical axis ranges, identical everything except the width of the box.

Slope is not a property of your data. It is a property of your data and the shape of the plotting region, and the shape of the plotting region is usually decided by whatever default your software had, or by how wide the column was in the report. Nobody chose it on purpose, which means nobody checked it.

The practical fix

Before you ship a figure whose message is “this is rising fast” or “this is flat”, resize the window and see whether your sentence survives. If widening the plot turns your alarming trend into a shrug, the trend was in the aspect ratio, not in the data.

The classical advice is to aim for average slopes near 45°, where the eye judges changes in slope most accurately. Treat it as a starting point, not a rule.

Binning

A histogram has a parameter you must supply, and the shape you see depends on it.

Three pictures of the same 400 commutes. The first is coarse enough that you can see something uneven but not what: two clumps or one lopsided lump, with a boundary that could be anywhere in a twenty-minute window. The second shows two clean, well-separated groups—this is the truth about the data-generating process, and I know that because I wrote it. The third is a picket fence of noise, and the eye immediately starts inventing structure in the gaps between bars that are one or two observations tall.

(A small R detail worth knowing: breaks is a suggestion. hist() moves the cut points to round numbers, so breaks = 3 gave you five bars. If you need exact control, pass a vector of cut points instead of a count.)

Too few bins hide structure. Too many manufacture it.

There is no formula that makes this decision for you. hist()’s default (the Sturges rule) is fine as a starting point and known to under-bin large samples.

Try several. Then report the one you would defend, and be aware you chose it. If the finding you are excited about appears at breaks = 40 and vanishes at breaks = 20 and at breaks = 80, you have not found a bimodality; you have found a bin width.

The same problem, wearing different clothes, shows up everywhere: the smoothing bandwidth in density(), the window length in a moving average, the number of categories you collapse a variable into, and the time unit you aggregate to. Daily data plotted monthly is smoother than daily data plotted daily, and it is smoother because you smoothed it.

Dual axes

Two series with different units on one plot, one axis on the left and one on the right. Extremely common in business reporting, and almost always a mistake.

The left panel says: enrollment and cost moved in lockstep for fifteen years. The right panel says: cost ran ahead of enrollment for a decade, and enrollment only caught up and overtook it around 2020.

Both are the same two series. The only difference is the range of the right-hand axis—and there is no correct range, because the two variables have no common unit. Where the lines cross, which one is “above”, whether they “diverge” in 2019: every one of those apparent facts is something the analyst chose, usually without noticing.

What to do instead
  • Two stacked panels sharing an x-axis. The reader compares timing and shape, which is what they wanted, and is never invited to compare heights that are not comparable.
  • Index both series to 100 at a base year and plot them on one axis. Now they share a unit—percentage of the base year—and crossings mean something.
  • Plot the derived quantity you actually care about. If the story is “cost is outpacing enrollment”, then cost per student is that story; plot it alone.

Aggregation, and the reversal it can hide

Now the most important idea in this chapter, and one of the two or three most important in the course.

Every aggregate—every mean, every rate, every bar in every summary chart—is computed over a group. Choosing the group is a modeling decision made before any modeling starts, and it can reverse your conclusion. Not weaken it. Reverse it.

A real trial

Two treatments for kidney stones, on 350 patients each. This is real data from a 1986 study, and it is famous for what it does.

Read the rate column:

  • Small stones: A cures 93.1%, B cures 86.7%. A wins.
  • Large stones: A cures 73.0%, B cures 68.8%. A wins.

A is better for small stones and better for large stones. Every stone is small or large. Now pool the two groups, which is what you do if you have not recorded stone size, or have and did not think it mattered:

B wins overall, 82.6% to 78.0%. Both statements are arithmetically correct on the same 700 patients. This is Simpson’s paradox.

Why it happens

There is nothing mystical here, and the resolution is worth working through slowly, because once you see the mechanism you will start spotting it in the wild.

Two things are true at once:

  1. Large stones are harder to cure, whichever treatment you use (73% and 69%, versus 93% and 87%).
  2. The treatments were not given to comparable patients.

Treatment A was given to large stones 75% of the time. Treatment B was given to small stones 77% of the time. Surgeons, reasonably, gave the more invasive treatment A to the harder cases.

So A’s pooled rate is mostly the large-stone rate, and B’s pooled rate is mostly the small-stone rate. The pooled comparison is not comparing treatments. It is comparing “A on hard cases” with “B on easy cases”, and calling the difference a treatment effect.

Make the mixes match and the reversal disappears. Here is A’s cure rate if A had been given to the same mix of stone sizes as B:

Given B’s patients, A would have cured about 88.5% against B’s 82.6%. The reversal was never about the treatments. It was entirely an artifact of who got which.

What Simpson’s paradox actually teaches

The lesson is not “always disaggregate”. Split finely enough and every subgroup has one patient in it and you learn nothing—and you can usually find some split that flips some result, which is a recipe for fooling yourself in the other direction.

The lesson is that the aggregate and the subgroup answer different questions, and you have to know which one you are asking.

  • “If I give this patient treatment A, what happens?”—you want the subgroup rate for that patient’s stone size. A is better.
  • “If I switch the whole hospital from B to A tomorrow, keeping the current triage practice, what happens to the overall cure rate?”—the pooled number is closer to relevant, precisely because it bakes in the case mix.

The data cannot tell you which question you meant. Only you can, and you must do it before you look, or you will pick the split that gives you the answer you wanted and call it an analysis.

It happens with numeric variables too

The same reversal occurs on continuous data, where it will show up in your regressions later in the term.

Pooled, y rises with x at a slope of about +2.5, and r = 0.92. Within every single group, y falls with x at a slope of about -2. Neither is a computational error. The groups sit at different heights and at different x positions, so the pooled line is tracking the difference between groups while the within-group lines track what happens inside one.

If those groups are hospitals, schools, or years, the difference between those two slopes is often the entire finding.

Simpson’s paradox

A university reports that men were admitted at a higher rate than women overall. Broken down by department, women were admitted at a higher rate in every single department.

Both sets of numbers were computed correctly from the same applications.

Which statement best explains this?

This is Simpson’s paradox, and there is no error in it. Each pooled rate is a weighted average of that group’s department-level rates, weighted by where that group applied. If women applied disproportionately to competitive departments with low admission rates for everyone, their pooled rate is dragged down by the mix, not by their treatment inside any department.

The reversal is a real feature of the arithmetic, so the first option is wrong. The third is wrong in a way worth naming: the pooled figure uses more rows, but more rows is not more relevant evidence—the extra rows differ in exactly the variable (department) that drives the outcome. And the fourth over-reads it in the opposite direction; the department-level advantage for women may itself reflect who applied where, and nothing here establishes a cause in either direction.

The right move is not “always disaggregate” but knowing which question you are asking. Was an individual applicant to Department X treated differently? is the department-level question. What was the overall admission rate by sex, given how applications were distributed? is the pooled question. Both are answerable; they are simply not the same question.

Exercise: undoing the reversal

(87 / 350) * (234 / 270) + (263 / 350) * (55 / 80)

This is standardization: comparing two groups on a common reference population instead of on their own. It is the standard repair for a Simpson’s reversal, and it is exactly what “age-adjusted mortality rate” means.

answer <- "B"

The subgroup that contains your patient is the subgroup that describes your patient. Sample size in an irrelevant subgroup does not help.

A short list of the rest

The same reasoning, applied quickly to other common choices:

  • Color. Roughly one man in twelve has some form of red-green color deficiency. A red-versus-green comparison is invisible to them. Vary lightness as well as hue, or use shape, or label directly.
  • Pie charts. People compare angles badly and lengths well. Almost any pie chart is a better bar chart. The exception is one slice against the rest, where the whole is genuinely the point.
  • Sorting. Bars in alphabetical order are almost never useful. Bars sorted by value make the comparison you presumably wanted the reader to make—but note that sorting is itself an argument about what matters.
  • Overplotting. With 50,000 points a scatterplot is a black rectangle and you cannot see density at all. Use transparency, or smaller points, or bin into a 2D histogram.
  • What is missing. A time series that starts in 2015 makes a claim by omission if 2014 was the peak. So does a bar chart of six countries out of thirty. Ask what a reader would want to see that is not there, and ask it of your own figures before somebody else does.
The chart as an argument

You plot monthly server response times for the past two years and the line looks alarmingly steep. You then widen the figure to the full page width and the same line looks nearly flat. The data, the axis limits, and the code are unchanged.

What is the right response?

Apparent slope is a property of the data and the plotting region, and the plotting region is usually inherited from a default nobody chose deliberately. Neither picture is the true one, so picking whichever supports the story you already have is exactly the failure mode this chapter is about.

The way out is to leave the picture for a moment. Response time went from what to what, over what period, and is that change large relative to what users notice or what your budget cares about? That is a claim in words with numbers attached, and it can be checked. Once you have it, the figure’s job is to communicate it faithfully, and the aspect ratio is chosen in service of that.

The last option is tempting and half right: the fitted slope genuinely does not depend on the figure’s shape, which is a real argument for reporting it. But drawing the line on the plot does not fix how steep the line looks—it inherits the same aspect ratio as everything else. The number helps; the added ink does not.

Summary

  • A chart is a sentence about the data, not a photograph of it. Ask what claim a reader will leave with, and whether you would write that claim in words.
  • Bars must start at zero, because bars encode length. Lines need not, because lines encode change. Match the axis to the comparison you are asking for.
  • Aspect ratio changes slope. Resize the figure and see whether your sentence survives.
  • Bin width changes shape. Too few bins hide structure; too many manufacture it. Try several, and know that you chose one.
  • Dual axes let you place the crossing point wherever you like. Use two panels, index both series, or plot the ratio you actually mean.
  • Aggregation can reverse a comparison when the groups have different compositions. Know whether your question is about an individual case or about a whole population before you choose the split—and consider standardizing.
  • None of these is a trick you avoid. They are decisions you make. Make them on purpose, and say what you made.
