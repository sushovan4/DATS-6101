The Central Limit Theorem


15 minutes

The Standard Error of a Mean told you where the sampling distribution of \bar X sits and how wide it is: centered at \mu, with spread \sigma/\sqrt{n}. Two numbers, derived in four lines, with no assumption about the population beyond having a finite variance.

What it did not tell you is the shape. And without the shape you cannot turn those two numbers into a statement, because “the estimate is 31.4 with a standard error of 0.28” does not yet say how often the truth is within half a point.

The central limit theorem supplies the shape, and it supplies it in a form so convenient that it is worth being suspicious of.

What it says

For independent observations from a population with finite mean \mu and finite variance \sigma^2, the sampling distribution of \bar X approaches a Normal distribution with mean \mu and standard deviation \sigma/\sqrt{n}, as n grows.

Read what is not in that sentence. There is no condition on the shape of the population. It may be skewed, bimodal, discrete, bounded, or something with no name. Provided the variance is finite and the draws are independent, the means go Normal.

That is a genuinely surprising claim, so watch it happen rather than believing it.

Watching it happen

The exponential distribution is about as un-Normal as a common distribution gets: it starts at zero, falls away immediately, and has a long right tail. Its mean and standard deviation are both 1 when the rate is 1.

Now take samples of size n, average each one, and look at the distribution of those averages.

At n = 1 you are looking at the population itself. By n = 5 the worst of the skew is gone. By n = 30 it is nearly symmetric, and at n = 100 you would not guess the parent distribution from the picture.

Put numbers on it. Skewness measures asymmetry: zero for a symmetric distribution, positive for a long right tail.

Two things at once. The center and spread are exactly what the previous chapter predicted—0.1 at n = 100, against 1/\sqrt{100}. And the skew falls from 1.91 to 0.19, roughly like 1/\sqrt{n}, which is the central limit theorem doing its work.

How large is large enough?

Textbooks say n = 30. Treat that as folklore rather than a theorem.

The honest answer is that the required n depends on how skewed the population is. A symmetric population is close to Normal at n = 5; the exponential above is respectable by 30; a distribution with a rare enormous value—insurance claims, revenue per customer, time on site—may still be visibly skewed at n = 1{,}000.

The population’s skew is about 8.9. At n = 30 the means are still more skewed than the exponential population was to begin with, and it takes n = 1{,}000 to reach the symmetry the exponential had at n = 30. The rule is not a sample size; it is to check. Simulating from a plausible population takes one line, and it is the only defensible way to answer the question for data you actually have.

What it does not say

Three misreadings, in order of how often they appear.

It does not say your data become Normal. Nothing happens to the data. The 20,000 exponential values in the first histogram are just as skewed as they ever were. It is the distribution of the mean that goes Normal, and the mean is a different object.

It does not say every statistic goes Normal. The theorem is about sums, and the mean is a sum divided by n. A median, a maximum, a correlation, a ratio— none of them are covered by it. This is the gap Why Resample exists to fill.

It does not license a Normal model for individual observations. If you need to say where the next single value will fall, the CLT is silent. It describes the estimate, not the world.

Where it fails

The theorem has one condition worth respecting: the population must have a finite variance. Distributions without one exist, and they are not merely pathological—financial returns, network traffic and file sizes are all modeled with heavy tails.

The Cauchy distribution is the standard example. It looks like a bell curve with fat tails, and it has no mean and no variance at all.

A hundredfold increase in sample size and the spread does not move. For a Cauchy population, the mean of 1,000 observations is no more precise than the mean of 10—averaging accomplishes nothing, because the occasional enormous draw dominates whatever the other 999 do.

You will not meet a true Cauchy. You will meet data with tails heavy enough that convergence is slow and the sample mean is a poor summary, and the practical signal is the same: if the mean of your data lurches around when you add observations, averaging is not working and no interval built on it will be honest.

Exercise: how skewed are the means?

The population is rexp(n, rate = 1). Return the skewness of 5,000 sample means at n = 25, using the function skew() provided.

skew(replicate(5000, mean(rexp(25, rate = 1))))

About 0.4, against a population skew of 2. The reduction is a factor of \sqrt{25} = 5, which is the general rate: skew falls like 1/\sqrt{n}.

That rate is the useful form of the “is n big enough” question. It tells you that a population twice as skewed needs four times the sample to reach the same symmetry—and that no sample size fixes a population with infinite variance, because there the argument does not start.

Summary

  • The CLT supplies the shape that The Standard Error of a Mean was missing: \bar X is approximately Normal, centered at \mu, with spread \sigma/\sqrt{n}.
  • It requires independence and finite variance, and nothing at all about the shape of the population.
  • Convergence was visible by n = 30 for an exponential population and incomplete at n = 1{,}000 for a heavily skewed log-normal. The number 30 is folklore; simulate instead.
  • Skew of the sampling distribution falls like 1/\sqrt{n}.
  • It is about the mean, not the data and not every statistic. Medians, maxima and correlations are outside it.
  • With infinite variance it fails outright: averaging 1,000 Cauchy draws is no better than averaging 10.
