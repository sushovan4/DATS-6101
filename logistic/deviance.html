Deviance and Checking


11 minutes

Linear regression had R^2 and a residual standard error. Logistic regression has neither, and the substitute is deviance—which is less intuitive, more useful, and printed on every glm summary whether you read it or not.

What deviance is

Deviance measures how far a model’s fitted probabilities are from the observed zeros and ones. Lower is better, it is on an arbitrary scale, and only differences between deviances mean anything.

Two are reported.

  • Null deviance — the fit of a model with only an intercept: one probability for everybody.
  • Residual deviance — the fit of your model.

The drop between them is what your predictors bought.

A drop of 244 for one degree of freedom. Under a null of “this predictor does nothing”, that drop follows a \chi^2 distribution with 1 df, where anything above about 3.8 is significant. So this is decisive.

That is the logistic analogue of the F-test, and it generalizes: to compare two nested models, fit both and compare their deviances against a \chi^2 with df equal to the number of extra parameters.

Adding noise drops the deviance a little—it always does, exactly as R^2 always rose—and the \chi^2 test correctly declines to be impressed.

AIC does the same job with a penalty built in, so it is comparable across non-nested models. Lower is better, and a difference of less than about 2 is not worth acting on.

There is no R^2

Several “pseudo-R^2” measures exist—McFadden’s, Nagelkerke’s, Cox–Snell—and they are not the proportion of variance explained, because a binary outcome has no variance to apportion in that sense. They are monotone transformations of the deviance drop, they are not comparable across datasets, and quoting one as if it were R^2 misleads.

If you want a summary of how well the model separates the classes, use a classification measure and say what threshold you used.

Classification is a separate decision

The model returns probabilities. Turning them into predictions requires a threshold, and that threshold is a business choice, not a statistical one.

Moving the threshold from 0.3 to 0.7 raises precision from 0.78 to 0.93 and drops recall from 0.92 to 0.77. Nothing about the model changed—only the cut-off, and which error you preferred to make.

Those two words are the ones you met in Conditional Probability and Bayes: precision is P(\text{event} \mid \text{flagged}), recall is P(\text{flagged} \mid \text{event}), and which matters depends on the cost of a false alarm against the cost of a miss.

Accuracy is a bad summary

Here the outcome occurs 56% of the time, so predicting “yes” for everyone gets 56% accuracy with no model at all. For a rare event it is far worse: at 1% prevalence, predicting “no” always is 99% accurate and completely useless.

Always report the base rate next to any accuracy figure, and prefer precision and recall—or the interval on a coefficient, if the question was inferential rather than predictive.

Separation

The failure mode particular to logistic regression: if a predictor separates the outcome perfectly, the likelihood has no maximum and the coefficients run away.

A coefficient of 23.6 with a standard error of 58,059. R warns that fitted probabilities of 0 or 1 occurred, and that warning is the diagnosis.

What it means: the data say the effect is infinite, because they contain no case that contradicts a perfect rule. Usually the cause is a predictor that encodes the outcome—a variable recorded after the event, or a proxy for it.

What to do: look for the leak first. If the separation is genuine and the sample is simply small, penalized methods such as Firth logistic regression give finite estimates.

Exercise: did the predictor earn its place?

Fit am ~ wt on mtcars with family = binomial and return the drop in deviance from the null model to the fitted one.

```{r}
g <- glm(am ~ wt, data = mtcars, family = binomial)
g$null.deviance - g$deviance
```

24.05 on one degree of freedom, against a \chi^2 cutoff near 3.84. Decisive.

anova(g, test = "Chisq") reports the same drop with its p-value, and is the form to use when you have several predictors and want them assessed in turn.

Summary

  • Deviance replaces R^2: lower is better, the scale is arbitrary, and only differences matter.
  • The drop from null to residual deviance is a \chi^2 test on the predictors— 244 on 1 df here. anova(fit, test = "Chisq") does it; AIC compares non-nested models.
  • Pseudo-R^2 is not R^2. Do not quote it as though it were.
  • Classification needs a threshold, which is a business decision. Moving it from 0.3 to 0.7 took precision from 0.78 to 0.93 and recall from 0.92 to 0.77.
  • Accuracy needs its base rate beside it, or it is meaningless.
  • Separation produces enormous coefficients and standard errors, and usually means a predictor is leaking the outcome.
